* Random variables and realizations

*Definition 1.* Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ and $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$ be measurable spaces. A map $X \colon (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$ is a random variable if

\[ X^{-1}(C) = \{ \omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \in C \} \in \mathcal{F} \quad \text{for every } C \in \mathcal{A} \]

For an outcome $\omega \in \Omega$, the value $x \coloneqq X(\omega)$ is a realization of $X$.

Measurability ensures that the inverse images of measurable sets are events to which probability can be assigned. For a real-valued random variable, the value space is $(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))$, where $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is the Borel sigma-algebra generated by the open intervals.

For two independent fair dice, use $\Omega \coloneqq \{1, \dots, 6\}^2$, $\mathcal{F} \coloneqq 2^\Omega$, and the uniform probability measure on the 36 outcomes. The function defined by

\[ X(\omega_1, \omega_2) \coloneqq \omega_1 + \omega_2 \quad \text{for every } (\omega_1, \omega_2) \in \Omega \]

compresses 36 outcomes into 11 possible sums.

* The law of a random variable

*Definition 2.* Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space, let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$ be a measurable space, and let $X \colon (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$ be a random variable. The law or distribution of $X$ is the function $\mathbb{P}_X \colon \mathcal{A} \to [0, 1]$ defined by

\[ \mathbb{P}_X(C) \coloneqq \mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(C)) = \mathbb{P}(X \in C) \quad \text{for every } C \in \mathcal{A} \]

Here $\mathbb{P}(X \in C)$ is shorthand for $\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(C))$. The law is also denoted $\mathbb{P}_X = \mathbb{P} \circ X^{-1}$ or $\mathcal{L}(X)$. Because inverse images preserve disjoint unions and $X^{-1}(\mathcal{X}) = \Omega$, the law $\mathbb{P}_X$ is a probability measure.

Thus $\omega$ is an outcome, $X$ is a fixed map, $x = X(\omega)$ is a realized value, and $\mathbb{P}_X$ describes the probabilities of all possible values.

* PMFs, CDFs, densities, and quantiles

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space. In this section, all random variables are real-valued and defined on this space. A random variable $X$ is /discrete/ if all its values lie in a finite or countable set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. Its probability mass function (PMF) is defined by

\[ p_X(x) \coloneqq \mathbb{P}(X = x) \quad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R} \]

By countable additivity, for every $C \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$,

\[ \mathbb{P}(X \in C) = \sum_{x \in C \cap S} p_X(x) \]

For the sum $X$ of two independent fair dice, we obtain, for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$,

\[ p_X(x) = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{6 - \lvert 7 - x \rvert}{36}, & x \in \{2, \dots, 12\} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases} \]

For a real-valued random variable $X$, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) is the function $F_X \colon \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1]$ defined by

\[ F_X(x) \coloneqq \mathbb{P}(X \leq x) \quad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R} \]

Every CDF is nondecreasing and right-continuous, tends to zero at $-\infty$, and tends to one at $+\infty$. Moreover, for every $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$,

\[ \mathbb{P}(a < X \leq b) = F_X(b) - F_X(a) \]

Let $X$ be a real-valued random variable on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$. A Borel-measurable function $f_X \colon \mathbb{R} \to [0, \infty)$ is a /probability density function/ of $X$ if, for every $C \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$,

\[ \mathbb{P}(X \in C) = \int_C f_X(t) \, dt \]

Consequently, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(t) \, dt = 1$, and for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$,

\[ F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t) \, dt \]

A density is defined at every real number, but the law of $X$ does not determine the density uniquely: changing its value at one point to another nonnegative number leaves all probability integrals unchanged. For any $x \in \mathbb{R}$ at which the chosen $f_X$ is continuous, we have $F_X'(x) = f_X(x)$. However, differentiability of $F_X$ alone does not force every choice of density to take the value $F_X'(x)$ at that point.

A density value is not a probability. If $X$ has a density, then $\mathbb{P}(X = x) = 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, and a density may exceed one.

For a real-valued random variable $X$ with CDF $F_X$, define the quantile function $F_X^{-1} \colon (0, 1) \to \mathbb{R}$ by

\[ F_X^{-1}(u) \coloneqq \inf \{ x \in \mathbb{R} : F_X(x) \geq u \} \quad \text{for every } u \in (0, 1) \]

We use $F_X^{-1}(1/2)$ as the /median/ under this convention. It is important to note that a CDF can have jumps and flat regions, so it need not possess an ordinary inverse.

* Transformations

Let $X$ be a real-valued random variable on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$, taking values in a finite or countable set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. Let $g \colon S \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function and define $Y \coloneqq g(X)$. Then $Y$ is discrete, and for every $y \in \mathbb{R}$,

\[ p_Y(y) = \sum_{\substack{x \in S : \\ g(x) = y}} p_X(x) \]

For a random variable with a density, we first express events involving the transformed variable in terms of the original variable. Let $X$ take values in $[0, 1]$, with density $f_X(x) \coloneqq 1$ for every $x \in (0, 1)$ and $f_X(x) \coloneqq 0$ otherwise. We call this distribution /uniform on/ $[0, 1]$. Define $Y \coloneqq X^2$. Then

\[ \{ Y \leq y \} = \{ X \leq \sqrt{y} \} \quad \text{for every } y \in [0, 1] \]

so, for every $y \in \mathbb{R}$,

\[ F_Y(y) = \begin{cases}
0, & y < 0 \\
\sqrt{y}, & 0 \leq y < 1 \\
1, & y \geq 1
\end{cases} \tag{1} \]

One density $f_Y \colon \mathbb{R} \to [0, \infty)$ is defined, for every $y \in \mathbb{R}$, by

\[ f_Y(y) \coloneqq \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}, & 0 < y < 1 \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases} \tag{2} \]

Integrating (2) over $(-\infty, y]$ recovers (1) for every $y \in \mathbb{R}$. The endpoint values $f_Y(0) = f_Y(1) = 0$ are a choice; other nonnegative values there give the same law. The quantile function is

\[ F_Y^{-1}(u) = u^2 \quad \text{for every } u \in (0, 1) \]

More generally, let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space, let $I, J \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be open intervals, and let $X$ be a real-valued random variable on this space with density $f_X$ and $\mathbb{P}(X \in I) = 1$. Further, let $g \colon I \to J$ be a strictly increasing bijection such that $g$ and $g^{-1}$ are continuously differentiable. Define $Y \coloneqq g(X)$ on $\{ X \in I \}$ and $Y \coloneqq 0$ otherwise. Then $F_Y(y) = F_X(g^{-1}(y))$ for every $y \in J$. Moreover, one density $f_Y \colon \mathbb{R} \to [0, \infty)$ of $Y$ is defined, for every $y \in \mathbb{R}$, by

\[ f_Y(y) \coloneqq \begin{cases}
f_X(g^{-1}(y)) \left\lvert \dfrac{d}{dy} g^{-1}(y) \right\rvert, & y \in J \\
0, & y \notin J
\end{cases} \tag{3} \]

Equation (3) follows by substitution in the probability integral.

* The empirical CDF

Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and let $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}$ be fixed observations. Define the empirical CDF by

\[ \widehat{F}_n(x) \coloneqq \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}_{\{ x_i \leq x \}} \quad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R} \]

For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $\widehat{F}_n(x)$ is the fraction of observations not exceeding $x$. The function $\widehat{F}_n$ is a right-continuous step function, with a jump of size $k/n$ at a value observed $k$ times. A new random sample changes $\widehat{F}_n$, while the model CDF remains fixed.

For every $u \in (0, 1)$, define the empirical quantile by

\[ \widehat{F}_n^{-1}(u) \coloneqq \inf \{ x \in \mathbb{R} : \widehat{F}_n(x) \geq u \} \]

In Python, set ~method="inverted_cdf"~ in ~np.quantile~ to use the generalized inverse.
