* Definition:  Binary operation $+$ on a set $E$
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:binary-operation
:LECTURE_REF: 1.1
:END:

A binary operation on set $E$ is a mapping

\begin{align*}
+ : E \times E &\rightarrow E \\
(x, y) &\mapsto x + y \\
\end{align*}

which produces an element of $E$ out of two elements from $E$.

** Questions

*** DONE Why is it called a binary operation?

Its called binary because of the number of inputs.

- 1 input:  unary
- 2 inputs: binary
- 3 inputs: ternary
- n inputs: n-ary

*** DONE What does the $\mapsto$ mean?

It stands for "maps to". Its specifies the rule of the function, where each individual input is sent to.

- $\rightarrow$: specifies the type signature
- $\mapsto$: specifies the rule of a function

*** DONE Important.

This definition defines what it means to be a *binary operation*, not $+$ (addition) specifically, it can be any other binary operation, such as multiplication, union, or function composition.

* Definition:  (Commutative) Group
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:commutative-group
:LECTURE_REF: 1.3
:END:

We say that $(E, +)$ is a group when $E$ is a set, and $+$ is a binary operation on $E$ satisfying:

1. associativity: $(x + y) + z = x + (y + z)$, $\forall x, y, z \in E$

2. identity element (aka. neutral element): there exists $0_E \in E$ such that $x + 0_E = x$

3. inverse element: for every $x \in E$, there exists $\tilde{x} \in E$ such that $x + \tilde{x} = 0_E$. We usually denote it by $-x$.

If, in addition, the elements commute, then we speak about a commutative group (aka. Abelian group):

4. commutativity: $x + y = y + x$, $\forall x, y \in E$

** Questions

*** DONE What does "commutative" mean?

From Latin, "commutare" means "to exchange". Thats it.

*** DONE What is the point of defining a group?

In any group, $a + x = b$ has a unique $x = -a + b$ solution. Existence comes from the identity, uniqueness comes from inverses. All four axioms are precisely what is needed to guaranty this.

* Definition:  Scalar multiplication
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:scalar-multiplication
:LECTURE_REF: 1.5
:END:

Scalar multiplication on $E$ over the field $\mathbb{K}$ is a map

\begin{align*}
\cdot : \mathbb{K} \times E &\rightarrow E \\
(\lambda, x) &\mapsto \lambda \cdot x \\
\end{align*}

which produces an element in $E$ out of scalar $\lambda$ and an input element $x \in E$.

** Questions

*** DONE What does it mean "Scalar multiplication on $E$ *over* $\mathbb{K}$"?

Whatever follows the "over" - is a source of a scalars, whatever preceeds the "over" - is what's being acted upon.

*** DONE Is $E$ a set and a part of the field $\mathbb{K}$ along with addition and multiplication?

No, $E$ is not part of the $\mathbb{K}$.

- $\mathbb{K}$ is a *field*: a set with its own addition $+_{\mathbb{K}}$ and multiplication $\cdot_{\mathbb{K}}$ satisfying field axioms.

- $E$ is a set (which will become an Abelian/commutative group, then a vector space): equipped with its own addition $+_{E}$.

Note that $\cdot$ is a *third operation*. Its essentially a *bridge*, connecting $E$ to $\mathbb{K}$. It lives neither in $E$, nor in $\mathbb{K}$.

|-------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------|
| Operation               | Type signature                                        | Lives in     |
|-------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------|
| $+_E$                   | $E \times E \rightarrow E$                            | $E$          |
|-------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------|
| $+_{\mathbb{K}}$        | $\mathbb{K} \times \mathbb{K} \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$ | $\mathbb{K}$ |
|-------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------|
| $\times_{\mathbb{K}}$   | $\mathbb{K} \times \mathbb{K} \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$ | $\mathbb{K}$ |
|-------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------|
| $\times$ (scalar mult.) | $\mathbb{K} \times E \rightarrow E$                   | bridge       |
|-------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------|

* Definition:  Compatibility between $\cdot$ and $+$
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:scalar-addition-compatibility
:LECTURE_REF: 1.6
:END:

We say that $\cdot$ is compatible with $+$ if and only if for all $x, y \in E$ and all $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$:

1. identity element of the scalar multiplication: $1_{\mathbb{K}} \cdot x = x$ ($1_{\mathbb{K}}$ being the multiplicative identity of $\mathbb{K}$)

2. compatibility of scalar multiplication with field multiplication:

   $$(\lambda \mu) \cdot x = \lambda \cdot (\mu \cdot x)$$

3. distributivity of scalar multiplication w.r.t. vector addition:

   $$\lambda \cdot (x + y) = \lambda \cdot x + \lambda \cdot y$$

4. distributivity of scalar multiplication w.r.t. field addition:

   $$(\lambda + \mu) \cdot x = \lambda \cdot x + \mu \cdot x$$

** Questions

*** DONE Are we talking about $\cdot_E, +_E$ or $\cdot_{\mathbb{K}}, +_{\mathbb{K}}$ or a bridge operations?

The definition works with the following operations:

- $+_{\mathbb{K}}$: fiels addition

- $\cdot_{\mathbb{K}}$: field multiplication

- $+_E$: vector addition

Scalar multiplication $\cdot : \mathbb{K} \times E \rightarrow E$ is a new bridge operation.

So each axiom uses operation based on the context. Example:

$$(\lambda \mu) \cdot x = \lambda \cdot (\mu \cdot x)$$

$\lambda \mu$ - uses a $\cdot_{\mathbb{K}}$, while RHS uses scalar multiplication.

*** DONE Is this ok to mix operations? i.e. using $\cdot$ without labeling $E$ or $\mathbb{K}$?

Yes, its a standard practice. Otherwise it would be a context overload.

*** DONE What is the point in compatability? What happens if $\cdot$ and $+$ are not compatable?

Without axioms, scalar multiplication is just some arbitrary function $\mathbb{K} \times E \rightarrow E$.

The compatability axioms are exactly what is needed so that the field structure and the Abelian/commutative group structure talk to each other. Without them you won't have a vector space, you would have a set with two unrelated algebraic structures bolted on.

*** DONE Compatible with $+_E$ or $+_\mathbb{K}$?

Both actually. Look for axioms 3 and 4.

* Definition:  Vector space
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:vector-space
:LECTURE_REF: 1.7
:END:

Given a field $\mathbb{K}$, a set $E$, a binary operation $+ : E \times E \rightarrow E$ and a scalar multiplication $\cdot : \mathbb{K} \times E \rightarrow E$. We call the triplet $(E, +, \cdot)$ a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space if and only if $(E, +)$ is a commutative group, and $\cdot$ is compatible with the binary operation $+$. That is, for all $x, y, z \in E$ and all $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$:

1. $(x + y) + z = x + (y + z)$

2. $\exists\, 0_E \in E$ s.t. $x + 0_E = x$

3. $\forall x \in E, \exists\, \tilde{x} \in E$ s.t. $x + \tilde{x} = 0_E$ (denoted by $-x$)

4. $x + y = y + x$

5. $1_{\mathbb{K}} \cdot x = x$

6. $(\lambda \mu) \cdot x = \lambda \cdot (\mu \cdot x)$

7. $\lambda \cdot (x + y) = \lambda \cdot x + \lambda \cdot y$

8. $(\lambda + \mu) \cdot x = \lambda \cdot x + \mu \cdot x$

** Questions

*** DONE Why do we call it a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space when the main work is with $E$?

Because the structure of $E$ hugely depends on the choice of $\mathbb{K}$. The same set $E$ with the same addition can be a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$, over $\mathbb{Q}$, over $\mathbb{F}_2$ - and these are genuinely different vector spaces (e.g. dimensions would be different).

*** DONE Why "$\cdot$ is compatible with the binary operation $+$" and not "operations" when we talk about both $E$ and $\mathbb{K}$ addition operations?

Good point. This is actually a slightly imprecise shorthand, since axioms 5-8 involves two different addition operations: $+_{E}, +_{\mathbb{K}}$.

* Definition:  $\mathbb{K}[X]$ and degree of a polynomial
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:polynomial-ring-degree
:LECTURE_REF: 1.10
:END:

The set of polynomials over $\mathbb{K}$ is defined as

$$\mathbb{K}[X] := \left\{ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_k X^k \;:\; a_k \in \mathbb{K} \;\text{and}\; \exists\, n \in \mathbb{N}_0 \;\text{s.t.}\; a_k = 0_{\mathbb{K}} \;\text{for}\; k > n \right\}.$$

The degree of a polynomial $p \in \mathbb{K}[X]$ is

$$\deg(p) := \max\{ n \in \mathbb{N}_0 \;:\; a_n \neq 0_{\mathbb{K}} \}.$$

** Questions

*** DONE Do I understand right that there would be +1 number of polynomials than its degree?

Rephrasing: "a polynomial of a degree $n$ would have $n+1$ coefficients" - yes, thats correct.

*** DONE Does the definition need to define what $X$ is?

No, and thats the point. $X$ is not an element of $\mathbb{K}$ or any set you plug the values into. Its a formal indeterminate - a syntactic placeholder. A polynomial $\sum a_k X^k$ is really just a sequence $(a_0, a_1, \ldots)$ thats eventually zero.

You should understand that there are two different polynomials: polynomial function and polynomial object.

Polynomial function is basically $p(x) = 3x^2 + 2x + 1$, a function, where you plug numbers.

Polynomial object is $p = 3X^2 + 2X + 1$, a finite sequence of $\mathbb{K}$ elements $(1, 2, 3, 0, 0, \ldots)$.

Imagine $\mathbb{R}^3$, thats $\mathbb{R}_2[X]$, just in other form.

*** DONE But what is the point of writing $X^k$ and not $X_k$ if $X$ is not a variable?

Convention.

* Definition:  Linear combination
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:linear-combination
:LECTURE_REF: 1.13
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space. Given vectors $x_1, \ldots, x_n \in E$, a /linear combination/ of these vectors is any vector of the form

$$v = \lambda_1 x_1 + \lambda_2 x_2 + \cdots + \lambda_n x_n, \qquad \text{with } \lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n \in \mathbb{K}, \text{ and } n \in \mathbb{N}.$$

** Questions

*** DONE Are vector spaces infinite?

Over an infinite field ($\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, etc) - infinite.

Over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q : |V| = q^n$ for dimension $n$ - finite.

* Definition:  Generating set (Spanning set)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:generating-set
:LECTURE_REF: 1.14
:END:

Let $\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, \ldots x_n\}$ be a finite subset of vectors of $E$ with $n \in \mathbb{N}$. We say that $\mathcal{X}$ a /generating set/ (or /spanning set/) of $E$ if any element of $E$ can be expressed as a linear combination of the elements of $\mathcal{X}$:

$$\forall\, x \in E, \; \exists\, \lambda_1, \ldots \lambda_n \in \mathbb{K} \;\text{s.t.}\; x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i x_i.$$

Equivalently,

$$E = \text{span}\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{n}.$$

** Questions

*** DONE Is there any big reason behind the $\mathcal{X}$ notation over $X$?

Purely conventional.

* Definition:  Linearly independent set (Free family)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:linearly-independent-set
:LECTURE_REF: 1.15
:END:

Let $\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, \ldots x_n\}$ be a finite subset of vectors of $E$ with $n \in \mathbb{N}$. We say that $X$ is a linearly independent set (or a free family) if the only linear combination that gives the zero vector is the trivial one:

$$\lambda_1 x_1 + \cdots + \lambda_n x_n = 0 \;\text{for some}\; \lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n \in \mathbb{K} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \lambda_1 = \cdots = \lambda_n = 0.$$

* Definition:  Basis
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:basis
:LECTURE_REF: 1.16
:END:

A set of vectors $\mathcal{B} = \{x_1, \ldots x_n\} \subset E$ is a /basis/ of $E$ if it is both generating and linearly independent.

** Questions

*** DONE Can it be that $\mathcal{B} = E$, meaning the notation $\mathcal{B} \subset E$ is a typo?

No. $\mathcal{B} \neq E$, because:

1. $E$ is a vector space, so $0_E \in E$.
2. $\mathcal{B} = E \implies 0_E \in \mathcal{B}$.
3. $1_{\mathbb{K}} \cdot 0_E = 0_E$ holds.
4. This contradicts the definition of linear independence.

* Proposition: Coordinate of a vector on a given basis
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:basis-coordinates
:LECTURE_REF: 1.17
:END:

Let $\mathcal{B} = \{x_1, \ldots x_n\} \subset E$ be a basis of $E$. Then for any vector $x \in E$ there exist unique scalars $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n \in \mathbb{K}$ such that

$$x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i x_i.$$

The tuple of scalars

$$[x]^{\mathcal{B}} := (\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)^{\top} \in \mathbb{K}^n$$

is called the coordinate vector of $x$ with respect to the basis $\mathcal{B}$, and the linear mapping

\begin{align*}
\text{Vec} : E &\rightarrow \mathbb{K}^n \\
x &\mapsto [x]^{\mathcal{B}}
\end{align*}

is a isomorphism.

** Questions

*** DONE Does $[x]^{\mathcal{B}}$ mean $x$ is defined through linear combination of a basis?

Not "defined through", but "represented as". The vector $x \in E$ exists independently of any basis. $[x]^{\mathcal{B}}$ extracts the unique coefficients that express $x$ in terms of $\mathcal{B}$.

Different basis, different coefficients, same vector.

*** DONE Is this a regular notation with $\mathcal{B}$ in $[x]^{\mathcal{B}}$?

Not really, heavily depends on the context.

*** DONE What exactly is $\mathrm{Vec}$ and why it was not used in definition?

Purely educational reason. $\mathrm{Vec}(x) = [x]^{\mathcal{B}}$ holds.

*** DONE What is isomorphism?

  The point is that isomorphism means the two spaces are "structurally identical" as a vector spaces. So once you fix a basis $\mathcal{B}$, your abstract vectors space $E$ becomes indistinguishable from $\mathbb{K}^n$ in terms of linear algebra.

*** DONE What does it mean to fix a basis?

To choose one basis and commit to it for the duration of the work.

* Proposition: Linearity of coordinate representation
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:coordinate-linearity
:LECTURE_REF: 1.18
:END:

Let $x, y \in E$. Then

$$[\alpha x + \beta y]^{\mathcal{E}} = \alpha [x]^{\mathcal{E}} + \beta [y]^{\mathcal{E}}, \quad \forall\, \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{K}.$$

#+begin_proof
We simply use the definition of the coordinate representation and the fact that $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a vector space. $\square$
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Subspace
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:subspace
:LECTURE_REF: 1.19
:END:

Let $(E, +, \cdot)$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space, and let $F \subset E$ be a subset of $E$. We say that $F$ is a subspace of $E$ if and only if $F$ is nonempty and stable by linear combinations:

1. $F \neq \emptyset$

2. $\forall\, x, y \in F$, $x + y \in F$

3. $\forall\, x \in F$, $\lambda \in \mathbb{K}$, $\lambda x \in F$.

** Questions

*** DONE What does "stable by linear combinations" mean?

Specifically the phrase "stable by" comes from French mathematics "stable par" which means "closed under". Hence, it meant $F$ is closed under linear combinations, which implies axioms 2 and 3.

* Proposition: Subspace characterization via closure under linear combinations
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:subspace-linear-combination
:LECTURE_REF: 1.20
:END:

$F \subset E$ is a subspace of $E$ if and only if:

1. $0 \in F$

2. $\forall\, x, y \in F$, $\forall\, \lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$, $\lambda x + \mu y \in F$.

** Questions

*** DONE Is this just another form of subspace validation (Definition 1.19)?

Yes, it just packs axioms 2 and 3 of *Definition 1.19* into one. Axioms 1 in both *Proposition 1.20* and *Definition 1.19* are equivalent as well.

* Proposition: Subspace intersection, union, and sum
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:subspace-intersection-union-sum
:LECTURE_REF: 1.22
:END:

Let $F$ and $G$ be two subspaces of a vector space $E$. Then:

1. $F \cap G$ is a subspace of $E$

2. $F \cup G$ is in general not a subspace of $E$

3. $F + G := \{x + y : x \in F, y \in G\}$ is a subspace of $E$, and we have the Grassmann formula

   $$\dim(F + G) = \dim(F) + \dim(G) - \dim(F \cap G)$$

** Questions

*** DONE This is a proposition, why there is no proof of proposed content?

True, lecture notes misses the proof of that points.

#+begin_proof
1. $F \cap G$ is a subspace: $0_E \in F$ and $0_E \in G$, so $0_E \in F \cap G$. If $x, y \in F \cap G$ and $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$, $\lambda x + \mu y \in F$ (since $F$ is a subspace), and $\lambda x + \mu y \in G$ (since $G$ is a subspace), hence $\lambda x + \mu y \in F \cap G$.

2. $F \cup G$ is in general not a subspace: Take $\mathbb{R}^2$, let $F$ be $x$ axis and $G$ be $y$ axis. Then, $(1,0) \in F \cup G$ and $(0,1) \in F \cup G$, but $(1,0) + (0,1) = (1,1) \notin F \cup G$.

3. $F + G$ is a subspace: $0_E = 0_E + 0_E \in F + G$. If $x_1 + y_1, x_2 + y_2 \in F + G$, with $x_i \in F, y_i \in G$, then $\lambda(x_1 + y_1) + \mu(x_2 + y_2) = (\lambda x_1 + \lambda y_1) + (\mu x_2 + \mu y_2) \in F + G$.
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Direct sum
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:direct-sum
:LECTURE_REF: 1.23
:END:

The sum of two subspaces $F$, $G$ of a vector space $E$ is said to be direct, and is written $F + G = F \oplus G$, when for any $x \in E$ there exists a unique decomposition

$$x = x_F + x_G$$

such that $x_F \in F$ and $x_G \in G$. This is equivalent to the condition $F \cap G = \{0\}$. The two subspaces are said to be complementary when their sum is direct and $E = F \oplus G$.

** Question

*** DONE Would it be more accurate to write $F \cap G = \{ 0_E \}$?

Yes, it would be more accurate.

*** DONE What is the point of a "complementary" definition, when the definition of a direct sum is enough ($\forall x \in E, x = x_F + x_G$)?

Yes, that is a non-standard definition. The standard approach separates two things:

- *Direct sum*: $F + G$ is derect iff $F \cap G = \{0_E\}$. This is purely about the uniquness of decomposition within $F + G$, which may be a proper subspace of $E$.

- *Complementary*: $F$ and $G$ are complementary iff $F \oplus G = E$, i.e. the direct sum exhausts the whole vector space.

* Definition:  Linear map
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:linear-map
:LECTURE_REF: 1.24
:END:

Let $E$ and $F$ be two $\mathbb{K}$ vector spaces. The mapping $f : E \rightarrow F$ is linear if and only if

$$f(\lambda x + \mu y) = \lambda f(x) + \mu f(y), \quad \forall x, y \in E, \; \lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$$

We denote by $\mathcal{L}(E, F)$ the set of all linear mappings from $E$ to $F$. When $F = E$, the mapping is called an endomorphism, and the set of endomorphisms is denoted as $\mathcal{L}(E) := \mathcal{L}(E, E)$.

** Questions

*** DONE What is the idea behind the map being linear? What if a map is not linear?

A linear map preserves addition and scaling. Map is fully determined by what it does to a basis, $f(\sum_{\lambda_i e_i}) = \sum \lambda_i f(e_i)$.

If the map is not linear, you loose all of that, $f(x + y) \neq f(x) + f(y)$. You wouldn't be able to use the entire machinery of linear algebra: rank, kernel, eigenvalues, etc.

* Proposition: Subspace and kernel/image structure of linear maps
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:linear-map-kernel-image
:LECTURE_REF: 1.25
:END:

Let $E$ and $F$ be two $\mathbb{K}$ vector spaces, and $f \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$. Then:

1. $\mathcal{L}(E, F)$ is a subspace of $\mathcal{F}(E, F)$ (with addition and scalar multiplication as described Sec 1.2.5), and this implies that $\mathcal{L}(E, F)$ is a vector space.

2. The kernel and image sets

   $$\ker(f) := \{x \in E : f(x) = 0\}$$

   $$\mathrm{im}(f) := \{y \in F : \exists x \in E \; \text{s.t.} \; y = f(x)\}$$

   are subspaces of $E$ and $F$ respectively.

** Question

*** DONE Would it be more accurate to write $\ker(f) := \{\forall x \in E : f(x) = 0\}$ (same idea for $\mathrm{im}(f)$)?

No, the set builder notation already implies that. So its unnecessary.

*** DONE What exactly is a $\mathcal{F}(E, F)$?

$\mathcal{F}(E, F)$ is a set of *all* functions from $E$ to $F$, not only the linear ones.

*** DONE Why last line says "are subspaces of $E$" when $\mathrm{im}(f)$ is subspace of $F$?

Thats a typo.

* Theorem:     Existence of dual basis
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:dual-basis-existence
:LECTURE_REF: 1.29
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space of finite dimension $n \in \mathbb{N}$, and let $\mathcal{E} = \{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ be a basis of $E$. For every $1 \le i \le n$, we define

\begin{align*}
e_i^* : E &\rightarrow \mathbb{K} \\
x &\mapsto [x]_i^{\mathcal{E}},
\end{align*}

which is the mapping that returns the $i$ th coordinate component of the elements of $E$ when expressed in the basis $\mathcal{E}$. Then $e_i^* \in E^*$ and

$$\mathcal{B}^* := \{e_1^*, \ldots, e_n^*\}$$

is a basis of $E^*$, which is called the dual basis of $\mathcal{E}$. Furthermore,

$$e_i^{*}(e_j) = \delta_{i,j}, \quad \forall \; 1 \le i, j \le n$$

#+begin_proof
The mapping $e_i^*$ is linear by [[#prop:coordinate-linearity][Proposition 1.18]], and the fact that component extraction is also a linear application. We next prove that $\mathcal{B}^*$ is a basis of $E^*$ by showing that it is both a linearly independent and generating set.

- Linear Independence: Let $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n \in \mathbb{K}$ such that $\lambda_1 e_1^* + \cdots + \lambda_n e_n^* = 0$. Then, $(\lambda_1 e_1^* + \cdots + \lambda_n e_n^*)(e_i) = \lambda_i = 0$ for all $1 \le i \le n$, which shows that $\mathcal{B}^*$ is a linearly independent set.

- Generating set: Let $\varphi \in E^*$. For every $x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} [x]_i^{\mathcal{E}} \; e_i \in E$, it holds that

  $$\varphi(x) = \varphi(x_1 e_1 + \cdots + x_n e_n) = [x]_1^{\mathcal{E}} \; \varphi(e_1) + \cdots + [x]_n^{\mathcal{E}} \; \varphi(e_n) = \varphi(e_1) e_1^*(x) + \cdots + \varphi(e_n) e_n^*(x),$$

  where we have used that $[x]_i^{\mathcal{E}} = e_i^*(x)$ by definition of the $e_i^*$. Since the equality holds for every $x \in E$, we obtain the equality of linear functionals

  $$\varphi = \varphi(e_1) e_1^* + \cdots + \varphi(e_n) e_n^*$$

  It expresses $\varphi$ as linear combination of the elements $e_i^* \in E^*$, thus showing that $\varphi \in \mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{B}^*\}_{i=1}^{n}$. Since this holds for every $\varphi \in E^*$, we have shown that $E^* = \mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{B}^*\}$.
#+end_proof

** Questions

*** DONE So $e_i^{*}$ returns a $i$ th coordinate component, but why $e_i^{*}(e_j) = \delta_{i,j}$, where $\delta_{i,j}$ is either $0$ or $1$?

Look closely, you are working with $e_j$, which is already an element of basis, so it should be either $0$ or $1$.

*** DONE I do not understand the logic behind indexing here: $e_i^{*}(e_j) = \delta_{i,j}$

Nothing to do with matrices. $i$ is responsible for elements of dual basis, while $j$ for regular basis.

* Definition:  Annihilator
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:annihilator
:LECTURE_REF: 1.31
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space of dimension $n$, and let $W$ be a subspace of $E$. The annihilator $W^{\circ}$ of $W$ is the set of linear functionals that vanish on $W$.

$$W^{\circ} := \{\varphi \in E^* : \varphi(x) = 0, \; \forall x \in W\}$$

* Theorem:     Dimension of annihilator
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:annihilator-dimension
:LECTURE_REF: 1.33
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space of dimension $n$, and let $W$ be a subspace of $E$. Then

$$\dim(E) = \dim(W) + \dim(W^{\circ}).$$

#+begin_proof
Suppose that $1 \le \dim(W) = k < n$, and let $(e_1, \ldots, e_k)$ be a basis of $W$. We complete this basis with $n - k$ vectors $(e_{k+1}, \ldots, e_n)$ to obtain a basis of $E$ which we call $\mathcal{B}$. We introduce the dual basis $\mathcal{B}^* = \{e_1^*, \ldots, e_n^*\}$, and observe that $W^{\circ} = \text{span}\{e_{k+1}^*, \ldots, e_n^*\}$. Indeed, we clearly have that $e_{k+1}^*, \ldots, e_n^* \in W^{\circ}$. In addition, any element $\mu \in W^{\circ}$ cannot have components in $e_1^*, \ldots, e_k^*$ since $\mu(e_1) = \cdots = \mu(e_k) = 0$. Therefore $\dim(W^{\circ}) = n - k$. $\square$
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Matrix representation of a linear map
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:matrix-representation
:LECTURE_REF: 1.34
:END:

Let:

1. $E$ and $F$ be two finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$ vector spaces (of dimension $p$ and $n$ respectively),

2. $\mathcal{E} = \{e_1, \ldots, e_p\}$ and $\mathcal{F} = \{u_1, \ldots, u_n\}$ be two bases of $E$ and $F$ respectively.

The mapping

$$\text{Mat} : \mathcal{L}(E, F) \rightarrow \mathbb{K}^{p \times n}$$

$$f \mapsto \text{Mat}(f) = [f]_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{F}} := \left( [f(e_1)]^{\mathcal{F}} \mid \ldots \mid [f(e_p)]^{\mathcal{F}} \right)$$

is an isomorphism. For every $f \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$, $[f]_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{F}}$ is called the matrix representation of $f$ in the bases $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{F}$.

#+begin_proof
We next prove that $\text{Mat}$ is both surjective and injective.

*Injectivity:* Let $f, g \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$ such that $\text{Mat}(f) = \text{Mat}(g)$. Then $[f(e_i)]^{\mathcal{F}} = [g(e_i)]^{\mathcal{F}}$ for all $i = 1, \ldots, p$. Since the coordinate representation is unique, it follows that this equality is also true at the level of the vectors, $f(e_i) = g(e_i)$. Let now $x \in E$ such that $x = \sum_{i=1}^{p} c_i e_i$. Then $f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i f(e_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i g(e_i) = g(x)$. Since this holds for all $x \in E$, it follows that $f = g$, and we have proven injectivity.

*Surjectivity:* Let $A \in \mathbb{K}^{p \times n}$. We are going to show that there exists $f_A \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$ such that $\text{Mat}(f_A) = A$. For this, we define a mapping $f_A : E \rightarrow F$ such that

$$[f_A(x)]^{\mathcal{F}} := A[x]^{\mathcal{E}}, \quad \forall x \in E.$$

The mapping $f_A$ is linear since, by construction, $[f_A(x)]^{\mathcal{F}} = 0_{\mathbb{K}^n}$ and therefore $f_A(0) = 0$. Also, for all $x, y \in E$ and $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$, $[f_A(\lambda x + \mu y)]^{\mathcal{F}} = A[\lambda x + \mu y]^{\mathcal{E}} = \lambda A[x]^{\mathcal{E}} + \mu A[y]^{\mathcal{E}} = \lambda f_A(x) + \mu f_A(y)$. This shows surjectivity. $\square$
#+end_proof

* Proposition: Evaluation equals matrix vector product
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:evaluation-matrix-product
:LECTURE_REF: 1.36
:END:

Let $f \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$ and let $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{F}$ be two bases of $E$ and $F$ respectively. Then

$$[f(x)]^{\mathcal{F}} = [f]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}} [x]^{\mathcal{E}}, \quad \forall x \in E.$$

#+begin_proof
Let $x \in E$, and denote $[x]^{\mathcal{E}} = (c_1, \ldots, c_n)^\top$ its coordinate representation in the basis $\mathcal{E}$. Then $f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^n c_j f(e_j)$, and by [[#prop:coordinate-linearity][Proposition 1.18]], $[f(x)]^{\mathcal{F}} = \sum_{j=1}^n c_j [f(e_j)]^{\mathcal{F}} = [f]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}} [x]^{\mathcal{E}}$. To obtain the last equality, we have used the definition of $[f]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}$ from Eq. (1.2), and we have observed that the linear combination can be expressed as a matrix-vector product.
#+end_proof

* Proposition: Matrix of linear combination equals linear combination of matrices
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:matrix-linear-combination
:LECTURE_REF: 1.37
:END:

Let $E, F$ be two finite $\mathbb{K}$ dimensional spaces, $f, g \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$, $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{K}$, and $\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{F}$ bases of $E$ and $F$ respectively. It holds that

#+begin_proof

#+end_proof

* Proposition: Matrix of composition equals matrix product
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:matrix-composition-product
:LECTURE_REF: 1.38
:END:

Let $E, F, G$ be three finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$ vector spaces, $f \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$ and $g \in \mathcal{L}(F, G)$, and $\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{F}, \mathcal{G}$ bases of $E, F, G$. It holds that

$$[g \circ f]^{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{E}} = [g]^{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{F}} [f]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}.$$

#+begin_proof
Let $x \in E$. On the one hand, by [[#prop:evaluation-matrix-product][Proposition 1.36]], we have that

$$[(g \circ f)(x)]^{\mathcal{G}} = [g \circ f]^{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{E}} [x]^{\mathcal{E}}.$$

On the other hand, using the nested structure of the composition, and again using [[#prop:evaluation-matrix-product][Proposition 1.36]], we also have that

\begin{align*}
[(g \circ f)(x)]^{\mathcal{G}} &= [g(f(x))]^{\mathcal{G}} = [g]^{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{F}} [f(x)]^{\mathcal{F}} = [g]^{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{F}} [f]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}} [x]^{\mathcal{E}}.
\end{align*}

Therefore,

$$[g \circ f]^{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{E}} [x]^{\mathcal{E}} = [g]^{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{F}} [f]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}} [x]^{\mathcal{E}}, \quad \forall x \in E,$$

which leads to the desired equality.
#+end_proof

* Proposition: Change of basis representation of endomorphisms
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:change-of-basis-endomorphism
:LECTURE_REF: 1.41
:END:

Let $E$ be an $n$ dimensional vector space, let $\mathcal{E} = \{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ and $\mathcal{F} = \{f_1, \ldots, f_n\}$ be two bases, and let $T \in \mathcal{L}(E)$. Then

$$[T]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{F}} = [\operatorname{Id}]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}} \, [T]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}} \, [\operatorname{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}}.$$

#+begin_proof
Let $f_j$ be the $j$ th basis element of $\mathcal{F}$. By [[#prop:evaluation-matrix-product][Proposition 1.36]], it holds that

$$[T(f_j)]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}} = [T]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}} [f_j]^{\mathcal{E}}.$$

In addition, by Eq. (1.5), we have that $[T(f_j)]^{\mathcal{E}} = [\operatorname{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}} [T(f_j)]^{\mathcal{F}}$. Inserting this formula into Eq. (1.6) and using the invertibility of $[\operatorname{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}}$, we deduce $[T(f_j)]^{\mathcal{F}} = [\operatorname{Id}]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}} [T]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}} [f_j]^{\mathcal{E}}$. We now assemble $[T]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{F}}$ by gathering all the vectors as matrix columns, and obtain

\begin{align*}
[T]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{F}} &= \left( [T(f_1)]^{\mathcal{F}} \mid \ldots \mid [T(f_n)]^{\mathcal{F}} \right) \\
&= [\operatorname{Id}]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}} \, [T]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}} \left( [f_1]^{\mathcal{E}} \mid \ldots \mid [f_n]^{\mathcal{E}} \right) \\
&= [\operatorname{Id}]^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}} \, [T]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}} \, [\operatorname{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}}.
\end{align*}
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Projection mapping

:CUSTOM_ID: def:projection-mapping
:LECTURE_REF: 1.42

Let $E$ be a vector space. A linear map $P : E \rightarrow E$ is called a projection mapping if and only if $P^2 = P$.

** Questions

The name comes from the geometric picture. If $P^2 = P$, then applying $P$ twice does the same thing as applying it once, meaning everything in the image of $P$ is already fixed by $P$.

* Definition:  Symmetry with respect to a subspace

* Proposition: Kernel and image of projection and symmetry
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:projection-symmetry-kernel-image
:LECTURE_REF: 1.48
:END:

Let $P_{F \oplus G}$, $S_{F \oplus G}$ be projection and symmetric mappings onto/with respect to $F$ associated to the direct sum $E = F \oplus G$. Then:

\begin{align*}
\ker(P_{F \oplus G}) &= G, & \operatorname{im}(P_{F \oplus G}) &= F, \\
\ker(S_{F \oplus G}) &= \{0\}, & \operatorname{im}(S_{F \oplus G}) &= E.
\end{align*}

#+begin_proof
We shall prove the first two set equalities by double inclusion.

1. ($\ker P = G$): For $x = x_G \in G$, from the definition of the projection mapping we have that $P(x) = P(0 + x_G) = 0$, therefore $G \subseteq \ker P$. Conversely, if $x \in \ker P$, we first decompose $x = x_F + x_G$, and from the fact that $P(x) = x_F = 0$ we know that $x = 0 + x_G = x_G \in G$, therefore $\ker P \subseteq G$.

2. ($\operatorname{im} P = F$): If $x \in \operatorname{im} P$, then there exists $y \in E$ such that $P(y) = x$, writing the direct sum decomposition $y = y_F + y_G$ we get that $x = P(y) = y_F \in F$, therefore $\operatorname{im} P \subseteq F$. Conversely, for $x \in F$, since $P$ is a projection, we get that $P(x) = \operatorname{Id}(x) = x$ (why?), which implies that $x \in \operatorname{im} P$, therefore $F \subseteq \operatorname{im} P$.

3. ($\ker S = 0$ and $\operatorname{im} S = E$): Observe that $S^2 = \operatorname{Id}$, therefore $S^{-1} = S$ and $S$ is an invertible linear application, consequently $\ker S = \{0\}$ and $\operatorname{im} P = E$.
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Distance function
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:distance-function
:LECTURE_REF: 1.49
:END:

Let $E$ be a nonempty set. A function $d : E \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is called a distance on $E$ if and only if:

1. positivity:

   $$d(x, y) \ge 0, \quad \forall x, y \in E,$$

2. non-degeneracy: for all $x, y \in E$,

   $$d(x, y) = 0 \Rightarrow x = y,$$

3. symmetry:

   $$d(x, y) = d(y, x), \quad \forall x, y \in E,$$

4. triangle inequality:

   $$d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z), \quad \forall x, y, z \in E,$$

5. reflexivity:

   $$d(x, x) = 0, \quad \forall x \in E.$$

* Definition:  Norm and normed vector space
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:norm-normed-space
:LECTURE_REF: 1.52
:END:

Let $(E, +, \cdot)$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space. A norm is a mapping $\|\cdot\|_E : E \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ satisfying:

1. positivity: for all $x \in E$, it holds that $\|x\|_E \ge 0$,

2. non-degeneracy: for all $x \in E$, if $\|x\|_E = 0$, then $x = 0_E$,

3. homogeneity: for all $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ and all $x \in E$, $\|\lambda x\|_E = |\lambda| \|x\|_E$,

4. triangle inequality: for all $(x, y) \in E$, it holds that $\|x + y\|_E \le \|x\|_E + \|y\|_E$.

$(E, \|\cdot\|_E)$ is called a normed vector space.

* Lemma:       Examples of normed vector spaces
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:normed-space-examples
:LECTURE_REF: 1.54
:END:

The following vector spaces can be endowed with norms and be seen as normed vector spaces:

1. We can endow $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{C}^n$ with different norms:

   $$\|(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\|_p := \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i|^p \right)^{1/p}, \quad 1 \le p < +\infty$$

   $$\|(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\|_\infty := \max\{|x_1|, \ldots, |x_n|\}.$$

2. We can endow the space of matrices $\mathbb{K}^{q,n}$ with the following norms:

   $$\|A\|_p := \left( \sum_{i=1}^{q} \sum_{j=1}^{n} |a_{i,j}|^p \right)^{1/p}, \quad \forall A \in \mathbb{K}^{q,n}, \quad p \ge 1,$$

   $$\|A\|_\infty := \max_{i,j} |a_{i,j}|.$$

3. The $\mathbb{R}$ vector space of continuous functions $\mathcal{C}([0,1]; \mathbb{R})$ over $[0, 1]$ and with values in $\mathbb{R}$ can be endowed with the following norms:

   $$\|f\|_p := \left( \int_0^1 |f(x)|^p \mathrm{d}x \right)^{1/p}, \quad 1 \le p < +\infty$$

   $$\|f\|_\infty := \max_{x \in [0,1]} |f(x)|.$$

   The 2-norm is usually called the Frobenius, Schur or Hilbert-Schmidt norm.

Showing that the above norms are indeed satisfying all necessary properties can become difficult for general values of $1 \le p < \infty$. The proof is easier for the cases $p \in \{1, 2, \infty\}$.

* Definition:  Norm equivalence
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:norm-equivalence
:LECTURE_REF: 1.55
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space with $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}$ and $\|\cdot\|_{\beta}$ be two norms on $E$. We say that $\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}$ and $\|\cdot\|_{\beta}$ are equivalent if there exists two constants $0 < \underline{c} \le \overline{c}$ such that

$$\underline{c} \|x\|_{\alpha} \le \|x\|_{\beta} \le \overline{c} \|x\|_{\alpha}, \quad \forall x \in E.$$

** Questions

*** DONE The definition of equivalence with two constants give impression that there exist norms such that their difference on those norms on same vector is enourmous. Gives Big O notation vibes. Am I right?

Yes, exactly. Two equivalent norms can disagree wildly in numerical value, but never worse than a constant factor. The sandwitch $\underline{c} \|x\|_{\alpha} \le \|x\|_{\beta} \le \overline{c} \|x\|_{\alpha}$ is $\|x\|_{\beta} = \Theta(\|x\|_{\alpha})$.

Concrete example in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$:

$$\|x\|_{\infty} \le \|x\|_2 \le \sqrt{n} \|x\|_{\infty}$$

*** DONE But I thought $\|\cdot\|_n : \forall n \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{\infty\}$ are the only existing norms.

Those are called p-norms, and yes, they are not the only ones. One example of other norm is an operator norm in Theorem 1.60.

* Theorem:     All norms are equivalent in finite dimension
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:finite-dimension-norm-equivalence
:LECTURE_REF: 1.56
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space with $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. Then:

1. If $E$ is finite dimensional, then all norms are equivalent,

2. If $E$ is infinite dimensional, then there always exists norms that are not equivalent.

* Definition:  Bounded linear mappings
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:bounded-linear-maps
:LECTURE_REF: 1.57
:END:

Let $(E, \|\cdot\|_E)$ and $(F, \|\cdot\|_F)$ be two normed vector spaces. A linear mapping $T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$ is called bounded if there exists a constant $C > 0$ such that

$$\|T(x)\|_F \le C \|x\|_E, \quad \forall x \in E.$$

The space of bounded linear mappings is

$$\mathcal{B}(E, F) := \{T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F) : T \text{ is bounded}\}.$$

** Questions

*** DONE Why constant is a capital letter? Is there any point behind it?

No deep meaning, pure convention. $c$ is just oftenly used for coordinates or coefficients.

*** DONE Could you please explain intuitively boundedness of a linear map?

A linear map can stretch vectors, but a bounded one stretches by at most a finite factor.

$\|T(x)\|_F \le C \|x\|_E$: the output is never more than $C$ times the input.

*** DONE $\mathcal{B}$ in $\mathcal{B}(E, F)$ looks very like basis. Do they relate?

No, $\mathcal{B}$ stands for "bounded".

* Theorem:     Operator norm on bounded linear mappings
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:operator-norm
:LECTURE_REF: 1.60
:END:

The space $\mathcal{B}(E, F)$ endowed with the operator norm

\begin{align*}
\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} &:= \sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|T(x)\|_F = \sup_{\|x\|_E = 1} \|T(x)\|_F = \sup_{x \in E - \{0_E\}} \frac{\|T(x)\|_F}{\|x\|_E}, \quad \forall T \in \mathcal{B}(E, F)
\end{align*}

is a normed vector space, and it holds that

$$\mathcal{B}(E, F) = \{T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F) : \|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} < +\infty\}.$$

In addition, for any $T \in \mathcal{B}(E, F)$, $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is the smallest constant $C > 0$ such that (1.9) holds.

#+begin_proof
We first prove the three equalities given in Eq. (1.11):

- We first show that $\sup_{\|x\|_E = 1} \|T(x)\|_F = \sup_{x \in E - \{0_E\}} \frac{\|T(x)\|_F}{\|x\|_E}$ for all $T \in \mathcal{B}(E, F)$. For this, let $T \in \mathcal{B}(E, F)$ and $x \ne 0_E$. Thus the vector $y := \frac{x}{\|x\|_E}$ has norm 1, and by linearity

  $$\|T(y)\|_F = \frac{\|T(x)\|_F}{\|x\|_E}.$$

  Consequently,

  $$\sup_{\|y\|_E = 1} \|T(y)\|_F = \sup_{x \ne 0_E \in E} \frac{\|T(x)\|_F}{\|x\|_E},$$

  and this shows the first equality that we want to prove.

- We next show that $\sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|T(x)\|_F = \sup_{\|x\|_E = 1} \|T(x)\|_F$ for all $T \in \mathcal{B}(E, F)$. Since $\{x \in E : \|x\|_E = 1\} \subseteq \{x \in E : \|x\|_E \le 1\}$ we immediately obtain

  $$\sup_{\|x\|_E = 1} \|T(x)\|_F \le \sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|T(x)\|_F.$$

  To show the reverse inequality, we let $x \in E$ be such that $\|x\|_E \le 1$ and $x \ne 0$, and define $y = \frac{x}{\|x\|_E}$ so that $\|y\|_E = 1$. By linearity,

  $$\|T(x)\|_F = \|x\|_E \|T(y)\| \le \|T(y)\|.$$

  Hence

  $$\sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|T(x)\|_F \le \sup_{\|y\|=1} \|T(y)\|.$$

  Combining the two inequalities yields the result.

We now show that $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is a norm in $\mathcal{B}(E, F)$. For this, let $T \in \mathcal{B}(E, F)$.

1. (positivity and definiteness): By definition, $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is a supremum of nonnegative numbers, so $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} \ge 0$. If $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 0$, then $\|T(x)\|_F = 0$ for all $\|x\|_E \le 1$, hence $T(x) = 0$ on the unit ball. For any $y \in E$ with $y \ne 0$, set $x := y / \|y\|_E$, so $\|x\|_E = 1$, and by linearity

   $$T(y) = T(\|y\|_E x) = \|y\|_E T(x) = 0.$$

   Also $T(0_E) = 0_F$, so $T = 0$. Conversely, if $T = 0$ then clearly $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} = 0$.

2. (absolute homogeneity): For any scalar $\alpha \in \mathbb{K}$,

   $$\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} = \sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|(\alpha T)(x)\|_F = \sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} |\alpha| \|T(x)\|_F = |\alpha| \sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|T(x)\|_F = |\alpha| \|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}.$$

3. (triangle inequality): For all $x$ with $\|x\|_E \le 1$,

   $$\|(T_1 + T_2)(x)\|_F = \|T_1(x) + T_2(x)\|_F \le \|T_1(x)\|_F + \|T_2(x)\|_F.$$

   Taking the supremum over $\{x : \|x\|_E \le 1\}$ gives

   \begin{align*}
   \|T_1 + T_2\|_{\mathrm{op}} &= \sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|(T_1 + T_2)(x)\|_F \\
   &\le \sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|T_1(x)\|_F + \sup_{\|x\|_E \le 1} \|T_2(x)\|_F = \|T_1\|_{\mathrm{op}} + \|T_2\|_{\mathrm{op}}.
   \end{align*}

The three norm axioms are satisfied, hence $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is a norm on $\mathcal{B}(E, F)$. We finish by showing that Eq. (1.12) holds. Let $T \in \mathcal{B}(E, F)$. By definition Eq. (1.10) of $\mathcal{B}(E, F)$, $T$ is bounded, therefore there exists $C > 0$ such that $\frac{\|T(x)\|_F}{\|x\|_E} \le C$ for all $x \ne 0 \in E$. Taking the supremum in $x \ne 0_E$ shows that $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} \le C < +\infty$, which in turn implies that $T \in \{T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F) : \|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} < +\infty\}$, and therefore $\mathcal{B}(E, F) \subseteq \{T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F) : \|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}\}$. To show the reverse inclusion, we let $T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$ be such that $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} < +\infty$. Therefore, by the property of the supremizer, it holds that $\frac{\|T(x)\|_F}{\|x\|_E} < \infty$ for all $x \ne 0 \in E$. In addition $\|T(0)\|_F = 0$. This shows that $T$ is bounded, thus a member of $\mathcal{B}(E, F)$, and concludes the proof.
#+end_proof

** Questions

*** DONE What exactly is $\|x\|_{E} \le 1$, $\|x\|_{E} = 1$?

$\|x\|_{E} \le 1 := \{x \in E : \|x\|_{E} \le 1\}$, its a unit ball.

$\|x\|_{E} = 1 := \{x \in E : \|x\|_{E} = 1\}$, its a unit sphere.

The theorem says the supremum over the ball equals to supremum over a sphere. This works because $T$ is linear: any vector inside the ball is a scaled down version of something on the sphere. $T$ can not produce larger output inside than on the boundary.

*** DONE Could you please explain $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ definition in a more understandable way rather than formula?

$\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is a maximum stretching factor of $T$. You feed in every unit vector, and take the biggest output.

*** DONE Do I understand right that $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ is basically a function on a function?

Yes, $\mathcal{B}(E, F)$ is a vector space of a bounded linear maps (functions), and $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}} : \mathcal{B}(E, F) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$.

* Lemma:       Boundedness and continuity
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:bounded-continuous
:LECTURE_REF: 1.61
:END:

For linear mappings between normed spaces $(E, \|\cdot\|_E)$ and $(F, \|\cdot\|_F)$ the following are equivalent:

1. $T$ is bounded,

2. $\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} < +\infty$,

3. $T$ is continuous, that is: for all $x \in E$, for all $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that for all $y \in E$ such that $\|x - y\|_E \le \delta \Rightarrow \|T(x) - T(y)\|_F \le \epsilon$.

Therefore

\begin{align*}
\mathcal{B}(E, F) &= \{T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F) : T \text{ is bounded}\} \\
&= \{T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F) : \|T\|_{\mathrm{op}} < +\infty\} \\
&= \{T \in \mathcal{L}(E, F) : T \text{ is continuous}\}.
\end{align*}

** Questions

*** DONE But doesn't $\mathcal{B}(E,F)$ definition through $T$'s continuity need to establish a relationship between epsilon and delta directly to prove it?

Not really, This belongs to the proof section. It should be $\delta = \dfrac{\epsilon}{\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}}$.

* Definition:  Algebraic vs topological dual
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:algebraic-topological-dual
:LECTURE_REF: 1.62
:END:

For a normed $\mathbb{K}$ vector space $(E, \|\cdot\|_E)$, there are two notions of dual spaces:

- The algebraic dual is

  $$E^* := \mathcal{L}(E, \mathbb{K}) = \{\ell : E \rightarrow \mathbb{K} : \ell \text{ is linear}\}$$

  as already defined in [[#def:linear-form-dual-space][Definition 1.27]].

- The topological or continuous dual is the space of bounded linear functionals,

  $$E' := \mathcal{B}(E, \mathbb{K}) = \{\ell \in E^* : \|\ell\|_{\mathrm{op}} < +\infty\} \subseteq E^*.$$

  By [[#thm:operator-norm][Theorem 1.60]], it is a normed vector space when endowed with the operator norm Eq. (1.11), which is often called the dual norm.

** Questions

They are not the same object.

- $E^{*}$: vector space of all linear functionals $E \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$

- $E'$: vector space of all *bounded* linear functionals $E \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$

Hence $E' \subseteq E^{*}$.
