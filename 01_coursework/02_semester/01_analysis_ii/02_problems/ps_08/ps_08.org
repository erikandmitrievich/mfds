* Problem 1 :critical_point:classification:
** Task
#+begin_quote
Consider $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ given by

\[
f(x, y) = \sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}.
\]

Determine all critical points and classify them into local minima, maxima, and saddle points.
#+end_quote

** Solution

\begin{align*}
\nabla f(x,y) &=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}} \\
\dfrac{y}{\sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}} \\
\end{pmatrix} \\
\nabla f(x,y) &= 0 \iff x = 0 \text{ and } y = 0 \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
\partial_1 f &= \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}}
& \partial_2 f &= \dfrac{y}{\sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}} \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
\partial_{11} f
  &= \dfrac{\sqrt{1+x^2+y^2} \;-\; x \cdot \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2+y^2}}}{1 + x^2 + y^2}
   = \dfrac{(1+x^2+y^2) - x^2}{(1+x^2+y^2)^{3/2}}
   = \dfrac{1 + y^2}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} \\
\partial_{22} f
  &= \dfrac{\sqrt{1+x^2+y^2} \;-\; y \cdot \dfrac{y}{\sqrt{1+x^2+y^2}}}{1 + x^2 + y^2}
   = \dfrac{(1+x^2+y^2) - y^2}{(1+x^2+y^2)^{3/2}}
   = \dfrac{1 + x^2}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} \\
\partial_{12} f
  &= x \cdot \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)(1+x^2+y^2)^{-3/2} \cdot 2y
   = \dfrac{-xy}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} \\
\partial_{21} f &= \partial_{12} f \quad \text{(Schwarz)} \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
H_f(x,y) &=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{1 + y^2}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} & \dfrac{-xy}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} \\
\dfrac{-xy}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} & \dfrac{1 + x^2}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} \\
\end{pmatrix} \\
H_f(0,0) &=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{1 + 0^2}{(1 + 0^2 + 0^2)^{3/2}} & \dfrac{-0}{(1 + 0^2 + 0^2)^{3/2}} \\
\dfrac{-0}{(1 + 0^2 + 0^2)^{3/2}} & \dfrac{1 + 0^2}{(1 + 0^2 + 0^2)^{3/2}} \\
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1 \\
\end{pmatrix} \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1 & x_2 \\
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1 \\
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1 \\
x_2 \\
\end{pmatrix}
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1 & x_2 \\
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1 \\
x_2 \\
\end{pmatrix} \\
&= x_1^2 + x_2^2 > 0 \\
&\implies (0,0) \text{ is a strict local minimum.}
\end{align*}

* Problem 2 :critical_point:lipschitz:gradient_descent:
** Task
#+begin_quote
Consider the function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ given by

\[
f(x, y) = \sin x + \cos y.
\]

(a) Determine all critical points and classify them as local minima, maxima, and saddle points.

(b) Show that $\nabla f$ is $L$ -Lipschitz continuous.

(c) Compute all critical points within $[0, 2\pi] \times [-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\pi]$ in Julia via gradient descent by selecting suitable initial vectors $(x_0, y_0)$.
#+end_quote

** Solution

*** (a)

\begin{align*}
\nabla f(x,y) &=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(x) \\
-\sin(y) \\
\end{pmatrix} \\
\nabla f(x,y) &= 0 \iff x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi \text{ and } y = m\pi, \quad \forall n,m \in \mathbb{Z} \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
\partial_1 f &= \cos(x)
& \partial_2 f &= -\sin(y) \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
\partial_{11} f &= -\sin(x)
& \partial_{22} f &= -\cos(y) \\
\partial_{12} f &= 0
& \partial_{21} f &= \partial_{12} f \quad \text{(Schwarz)} \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
H_f(x,y) &=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\sin(x) & 0 \\
0 & -\cos(y) \\
\end{pmatrix} \\
H_f\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi,\; m\pi\right) &=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi\right) & 0 \\
0 & -\cos(m\pi) \\
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
(-1)^{n+1} & 0 \\
0 & (-1)^{m+1} \\
\end{pmatrix} \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1 & x_2 \\
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
(-1)^{n+1} & 0 \\
0 & (-1)^{m+1} \\
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1 \\
x_2 \\
\end{pmatrix}
&= (-1)^{n+1} x_1^2 + (-1)^{m+1} x_2^2 \\
\end{align*}

Case analysis on the parities of $n$ and $m$:
\begin{align*}

n \text{ odd},\; m \text{ odd}:\quad
& x_1^2 + x_2^2 > 0
&&\implies \left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi,\; m\pi\right) \text{ is a strict local minimum.} \\
n \text{ even},\; m \text{ even}:\quad
& -x_1^2 - x_2^2 < 0
&&\implies \left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi,\; m\pi\right) \text{ is a strict local maximum.} \\
n \text{ odd},\; m \text{ even}:\quad
& x_1^2 - x_2^2 \text{ indefinite}
&&\implies \left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi,\; m\pi\right) \text{ is a saddle point.} \\
n \text{ even},\; m \text{ odd}:\quad
& -x_1^2 + x_2^2 \text{ indefinite}
&&\implies \left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi,\; m\pi\right) \text{ is a saddle point.} \\
\end{align*}

*** (b)

\begin{align*}
\nabla f(x,y) - \nabla f(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}) &=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(x) - \cos(\tilde{x}) \\
-\sin(y) + \sin(\tilde{y}) \\
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}

By the mean value theorem applied to $\sin$ and $\cos$ on $\mathbb{R}$, together with $|\sin'| = |\cos| \leq 1$ and $|\cos'| = |\sin| \leq 1$:

\begin{align*}
|\cos(x) - \cos(\tilde{x})| &\leq |x - \tilde{x}| \\
|\sin(y) - \sin(\tilde{y})| &\leq |y - \tilde{y}|
\end{align*}

Hence

\begin{align*}
\|\nabla f(x,y) - \nabla f(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y})\|^2
&= (\cos(x) - \cos(\tilde{x}))^2 + (\sin(y) - \sin(\tilde{y}))^2 \\
&\leq (x - \tilde{x})^2 + (y - \tilde{y})^2 \\
&= \|(x,y) - (\tilde{x}, \tilde{y})\|^2.
\end{align*}

Therefore $\nabla f$ is $L$ -Lipschitz with $L = 1$. $\blacksquare$

let
    ∇f(x, y) = (cos(x), -sin(y))

    function gd(∇F, x0, y0; η=1.0, tol=1e-10, maxiter=10_000)
        x, y = x0, y0
        for _ in 1:maxiter
            gx, gy = ∇F(x, y)
            x -= η * gx
            y -= η * gy
            hypot(gx, gy) < tol && break
        end
        (x, y)
    end

    ∇f_neg(x, y) = .-∇f(x, y)
    ∇f_xmin_ymax(x, y) = (cos(x),  sin(y))   # min in x, max in y
    ∇f_xmax_ymin(x, y) = (-cos(x), -sin(y))  # max in x, min in y

* Problem 3 :newton_method:linearization:
** Task
#+begin_quote
Let $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuously differentiable. The Newton iteration is defined by
\[
x_{k+1} := x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}, \quad k \in \mathbb{N},
\]
whenever $f'(x_k) \neq 0$.

Justify the following interpretation of Newton's method:

In the $k$ -th Newton iteration, the function $f$ is replaced by its affine linear approximation $F_k$ at $x_k$. The next iterate $x_{k+1}$ is then defined as the solution of the linearized equation $F_k(x) = 0$.

More precisely:

1. Determine the affine linear approximation $F_k$ of $f$ at $x_k$.
2. Show that solving $F_k(x) = 0$ yields exactly the Newton iteration formula.
#+end_quote

** Solution

*** 1. Affine linear approximation of $f$ at $x_k$

The affine linear approximation (first-order Taylor polynomial) of $f$ at $x_k$ is

\begin{align*}
F_k(x) &:= f(x_k) + f'(x_k)(x - x_k).
\end{align*}

*** 2. Solving $F_k(x) = 0$

Assume $f'(x_k) \neq 0$. Then

\begin{align*}
F_k(x) = 0
&\iff f(x_k) + f'(x_k)(x - x_k) = 0 \\
&\iff f'(x_k)(x - x_k) = -f(x_k) \\
&\iff x - x_k = -\dfrac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)} \\
&\iff x = x_k - \dfrac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}.
\end{align*}

Defining $x_{k+1} := x$ recovers the Newton iteration. $\blacksquare$

* Problem 4 :newton_method:convergence:julia:
** Task
#+begin_quote
Write a Julia function

=myNewton(f, x0, tol, maxiter)=

that performs Newton's method for a function $f$, starting from the initial value $x_0$. The iteration should terminate when either

$$|x_{k+1} - x_k| < \text{tol}$$

or when the maximal number of iterations maxiter is reached. Your function should return:
- the final approximation,
- the number of iterations used,
- and a vector containing all iterates.

Test your implementation on the equations $x^2 - 2 = 0$ and $\cos(x) - x = 0$. Experiment with different initial values $x_0$. What do you observe? Plot the convergence history $k \mapsto |x_k - x^*|$ for one suitable example, where $x^*$ denotes the computed root.
#+end_quote

** Solution

#+begin_src julia :results output :exports both :tangle julia/p_04.jl
using Plots

function myNewton(f, df, x0, tol, maxiter)
    xs = [float(x0)]
    for k in 1:maxiter
        x = xs[end]
        x_new = x - f(x) / df(x)
        push!(xs, x_new)
        abs(x_new - x) < tol && return (x_new, k, xs)
    end
    (xs[end], maxiter, xs)
end

# --- Test 1: x^2 - 2 = 0, roots ±√2 ---
f1(x)  = x^2 - 2
df1(x) = 2x

for x0 in (2.0, -2.0, 0.5, 100.0)
    x★, k, _ = myNewton(f1, df1, x0, 1e-14, 100)
    println("f1, x0=$x0:  x★=$x★, iters=$k")
end

# --- Test 2: cos(x) - x = 0, root ≈ 0.739085 ---
f2(x)  = cos(x) - x
df2(x) = -sin(x) - 1

for x0 in (0.0, 1.0, -5.0, 10.0)
    x★, k, _ = myNewton(f2, df2, x0, 1e-14, 100)
    println("f2, x0=$x0:  x★=$x★, iters=$k")
end

# --- Convergence history for f1, x0 = 2.0 ---

*** Observations

- *Quadratic convergence.* For both equations, the error roughly squares each step: $|x_{k+1} - x^*| \approx C \, |x_k - x^*|^2$. On a log plot the error drops with doubling slope until machine precision saturates around $10^{-16}$.

- *Sign of $x_0$ selects the root for $f_1$.* Newton on $x^2 - 2$ converges to $\sqrt{2}$ for $x_0 > 0$ and to $-\sqrt{2}$ for $x_0 < 0$. The iteration $x_{k+1} = (x_k + 2/x_k)/2$ preserves sign and is a contraction toward the same-sign root.

- *Failure at $x_0 = 0$ for $f_1$.* $f_1'(0) = 0$, so the update divides by zero. The course problem doesn't ask to handle this, but it's worth noting since the instructions only guarantee the iteration "whenever $f'(x_k) \neq 0$".

- *Robustness for $f_2$.* $f_2'(x) = -\sin(x) - 1 \in [-2, 0]$, vanishing only at $x = \pi/2 + 2\pi n$. Away from those points Newton converges to the unique root $x^* \approx 0.739085$ from essentially any reasonable $x_0$. Starting *at* one of those points triggers division by zero.

- *Far-from-root behavior.* For $x_0 = 100$ on $f_1$, Newton spends several iterations roughly halving $x_k$ (the linearization is poor far from the root) before entering the quadratic regime. This is generic: quadratic convergence is a *local* phenomenon; the first few steps can be slow.
