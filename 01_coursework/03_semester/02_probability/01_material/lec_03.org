* Conditional probability

Let $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(B) > 0$. The conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)} \]

Conditioning restricts the relevant outcomes to $B$, then renormalizes their total probability to one. For a finite uniform law,

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\lvert A \cap B \rvert}{\lvert B \rvert} \]

For each fixed $B$ with positive probability, the map $A \mapsto \mathbb{P}(A \mid B)$ is itself a probability measure on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$.

*Example 1 (Two dice).* Roll two fair dice. If $A$ is the event that both dice show six and $B$ the event that at least one die shows six, then

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{1}{11} \]

The new information changes the relevant universe from 36 outcomes to the 11 outcomes in $B$.

* Multiplication and chain rules

Rearranging the definition gives

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A \mid B)\mathbb{P}(B) \]

If both conditioning directions are defined, then

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B)\mathbb{P}(B) = \mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\mathbb{P}(A) \]

More generally, provided the conditioning events have positive probability,

\[ \mathbb{P}\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = \mathbb{P}(A_1) \prod_{i=2}^{n} \mathbb{P}\left( A_i \mid \bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1} A_j \right) \]

#+begin_proof
The proof follows by induction. For $n = 2$, the statement is the multiplication rule. For the induction step, note that

\begin{align*}
\mathbb{P}\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n+1} A_i \right) &= \mathbb{P}\left( \left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \cap A_{n+1} \right) \\
&= \mathbb{P}\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \mathbb{P}\left( A_{n+1} \mid \bigcap_{j=1}^{n} A_j \right) \\
&= \mathbb{P}(A_1) \prod_{i=2}^{n} \mathbb{P}\left( A_i \mid \bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1} A_j \right) \mathbb{P}\left( A_{n+1} \mid \bigcap_{j=1}^{n} A_j \right)
\end{align*}

The second equality is the multiplication rule, and the last equality follows from the induction hypothesis. $\blacksquare$
#+end_proof

For example, an urn contains five red and seven blue balls. Without replacement, if $R_i$ denotes red on draw $i$, then

\[ \mathbb{P}(R_1 \cap R_2) = \mathbb{P}(R_2 \mid R_1)\mathbb{P}(R_1) = \frac{4}{11} \cdot \frac{5}{12} \]

* Total probability and Bayes' theorem

Events $B_1, \dots, B_K \in \mathcal{F}$ form a partition of $\Omega$ if they are pairwise disjoint and

\[ \Omega = \bigcup_{j=1}^{K} B_j \]

If every partition cell has positive probability, then

\[ \mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \mathbb{P}(A \mid B_j)\mathbb{P}(B_j) \]

Indeed, the events $A \cap B_j$ are pairwise disjoint and have union $A$.

For $\mathbb{P}(A) > 0$, Bayes' theorem gives

\[ \mathbb{P}(B_j \mid A) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(A \mid B_j)\mathbb{P}(B_j)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K} \mathbb{P}(A \mid B_\ell)\mathbb{P}(B_\ell)} \]

For the special partition $(A, A^c)$, let $B$ be an event with $\mathbb{P}(B) > 0$, and assume $0 < \mathbb{P}(A) < 1$. Bayes' theorem becomes the binary Bayes rule

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\mathbb{P}(A)}{\mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B \mid A^c)\mathbb{P}(A^c)} \]

The two terms in the denominator are the two mutually exclusive routes by which $B$ can occur.

** Diagnostic-test example

Let $D$ denote a condition and $T^+$ a positive test. This is the binary formula with $A = D$ and $B = T^+$. Suppose

\[ \mathbb{P}(D) = 0.01, \quad \mathbb{P}(T^+ \mid D) = 0.95, \quad \mathbb{P}(T^+ \mid D^c) = 0.10 \]

The partition $(D, D^c)$ gives

\[ \mathbb{P}(T^+) = 0.95 \cdot 0.01 + 0.10 \cdot 0.99 = 0.1085 \]

Therefore

\[ \mathbb{P}(D \mid T^+) = \frac{0.95 \cdot 0.01}{0.95 \cdot 0.01 + 0.10 \cdot 0.99} \approx 0.0876 \]

Equivalently, in a model population of 10 000, there are 95 true positives and 990 false positives. Thus $95/1085$ positive tests come from $D$.

* Independence

*Definition 1 (Independent events).* Events $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ are independent, written $A \perp\!\!\!\perp B$, if

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B) \]

If $\mathbb{P}(B) > 0$, this is equivalent to

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \mathbb{P}(A) \]

Thus observing $B$ does not change the modeled probability of $A$. If $A \perp\!\!\!\perp B$, then each of $A^c \perp\!\!\!\perp B$, $A \perp\!\!\!\perp B^c$, and $A^c \perp\!\!\!\perp B^c$ also holds.

Independence is different from disjointness. If $A \cap B = \emptyset$ and both events have positive probability, then

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = 0 \neq \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B) \]

so the events are not independent.

In the urn model, $R_1$ and $R_2$ are independent with replacement because

\[ \mathbb{P}(R_2 \mid R_1) = \frac{5}{12} = \mathbb{P}(R_2) \]

They are dependent without replacement because $4/11 \neq 5/12$.

** Pairwise versus mutual independence

Events $A_1, \dots, A_K$ are mutually independent if, for every nonempty $J \subseteq \{1, \dots, K\}$,

\[ \mathbb{P}\left( \bigcap_{j \in J} A_j \right) = \prod_{j \in J} \mathbb{P}(A_j) \]

Pairwise independence requires this equality only for pairs.

For two fair coin tosses, let $A$ be first toss heads, $B$ second toss heads, and $C$ that both tosses agree. Each event has probability $1/2$, and each pairwise intersection has probability $1/4$. However,

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \cap B \cap C) = \frac{1}{4} \neq \frac{1}{8} = \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\mathbb{P}(C) \]

The three events are pairwise independent but not mutually independent.
