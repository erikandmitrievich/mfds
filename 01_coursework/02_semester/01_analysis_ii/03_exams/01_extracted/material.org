We do not redefine differentiability — it really makes sense if you recall differentiability from the first semester, taking first derivatives in the sense that this is a linear approximation. In the first semester you could keep just this line in mind: $f(x + h)$. #+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $V$ and $W$ be normed vector spaces. A function $f : V \rightarrow W$ is called Frechet differentiable at $x \in V$ if there exists a continuous linear map $f'(x) : V \rightarrow W$ such that

$$f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x) h + o(\|h\|), \quad h \in V$$

Since $f'(x)$ is required to be continuous, every Frechet differentiable function is continuous.
#+end_quote

* Definition: Local extrema
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:local-extrema
:SOURCE: ch_04 §4.4.1
:LECTURE: 01
:END:

#+begin_quote
At the beginning, for the sheets, we have a few necessary conditions for general normed spaces, when we have a Fréchet derivative. So, local minimum — I think it is clear what you mean by it — and local maximum as well.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $U \subseteq V$ be open and let $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. A point $x_0 \in U$ is called

1. a local minimum if there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that

   $$f(x_0) \le f(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in B_\varepsilon(x_0),$$

   a strict local minimum refers to a strict inequality for $x \in B_\varepsilon(x_0) \setminus \{ x_0 \}$,

2. a local maximum if there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that

   $$f(x_0) \ge f(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in B_\varepsilon(x_0),$$

   a strict local maximum refers to a strict inequality for $x \in B_\varepsilon(x_0) \setminus \{ x_0 \}$,

If $x_0$ is either a (strict) local minimum or a (strict) local maximum, it is called a (strict) local extremum.
#+end_quote

* Theorem: Necessary conditions of local extrema
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:necessary-conditions-local-extrema
:SOURCE: ch_04 §4.4.3
:LECTURE: 01
:END:

#+begin_quote
The main difference is that no matter what strange space you have defined your function on — some normed vector space — if $f$ is Fréchet differentiable and it has a local minimum, then the first derivative is $0$. This is a general statement. And we will not only have necessary conditions but also sufficient conditions: if the first derivative is $0$, I can check whether it is indeed a local extremum — anything global would be even better.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $V$ be a normed vector space, $U \subseteq V$ an open subset, and let $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be Fréchet differentiable in $x_0 \in U$. If $x_0 \in U$ is a local extremum of $f$, then

$$f'(x_0) = 0.$$

Since $f'(x_0)$ is a linear map, the condition $f'(x_0) = 0$ means that

$$f'(x_0) v = 0 \quad \text{for all } v \in V.$$

The proof reduces the infinite-dimensional problem to a one-dimensional calculus argument by restricting $f$ to lines through $x_0$.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
So you should really be familiar with what a convex function means. What it really means: you have two points $x$ and $y$, you can draw the line between the two points, and the function is called convex if it stays below that line. You express the line from $x$ to $y$ as $t x + (1-t) y$: when $t = 0$ you have $y$, and when $t = 1$ you have $x$, and this is exactly the line between the two points. $$f(tx + (1 - t)y) \le tf(x) + (1 - t)f(y).$$

* Definition: Length of a curve in a metric space
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:curve-length
:SOURCE: ch_05 §5.0.1
:LECTURE: 01
:END:

#+begin_quote
We looked at curves, we looked at the length of a curve, and we looked at the derivative. We were able to compute the length of a curve — that is also a popular analysis exam question: given a curve, compute its length.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space and let $\gamma : \left[ 0, 1 \right] \rightarrow X$ be a curve. For a partition

$$\mathcal{P} = \{ 0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = 1 \}$$

of $\left[ 0, 1 \right]$, define the polygonal length of $\gamma$ along $\mathcal{P}$ by

$$\ell(\gamma, \mathcal{P}) := \sum_{j=1}^{n} d\left( \gamma(t_{j-1}), \gamma(t_j) \right).$$

The length of $\gamma$ is

$$\ell(\gamma) := \sup_{\mathcal{P}} \ell(\gamma, \mathcal{P}),$$

where the supremum is taken over all partitions $\mathcal{P}$ of $\left[ 0, 1 \right]$. The curve $\gamma$ is called rectifiable if $\ell(\gamma) < \infty$.
#+end_quote

* Definition: Partial derivatives
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:partial-derivatives
:SOURCE: ch_05 §5.1.8
:LECTURE: 01
:END:

#+begin_quote
Eventually we look at a map and assume it comes in coordinates, and then suddenly we have partial derivatives, which require more bookkeeping than the general Fréchet derivative. This you should be familiar with. The gradient is a vector of partial derivatives.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
The function $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is called partially differentiable at $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ if, for each $j = 1, \ldots, n$, the limit

$$\partial_j f(x) := \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x + h e_j) - f(x)}{h}$$

exists. The row vector

$$\operatorname{grad} f(x) := \left( \partial_1 f(x), \ldots, \partial_n f(x) \right)$$

is called the gradient of $f$ at $x$. Its transpose is a column vector and denoted by

$$\nabla f(x) = \operatorname{grad} f(x)^\top.$$
#+end_quote

* Theorem: Taylor formula in $\mathbb{R}^n$

#+begin_quote
It is unthinkable to have an analysis exam in the second semester with no questions about Taylor's formula. If that happens, raise your arms — something has gone wrong. In the first semester it is not so dominant, but almost everything we do in analysis goes back to Taylor's formula. In the second semester this should become clearer.

When you write it this way, where $\alpha$ is allowed to be a vector — $\alpha$ is a multi-index with $n$ components. [Dialogue.] The partial derivative with respect to a coordinate is the one-dimensional derivative in that coordinate; that is true. With $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)$, you take the partial derivative with respect to the first coordinate $\alpha_1$ times, and so on.

[Student: shouldn't $\alpha$ have only $k$ elements, because $f$ is $k$ times continuously differentiable?] The entries: I am only allowed to take the derivative $k$ times in total, $|\alpha| \le k$. But the length of $\alpha$ is always $n$, because I always specify how often I differentiate with respect to each of the $n$ coordinates.

I would definitely ask: please compute the Taylor expansion of this function of order $5$. So $k = 5$. Then you do all the combinations with $|\alpha| \le 5$ and compute the partial derivatives — I would only ask in cases where many of them are zero, otherwise it takes forever.

The proof was the standard idea: define a new function that only depends on one variable and apply Taylor's formula for one variable. For the second-order Taylor expansion — another classic exam question. If it is only order $2$, it is often easier to use the explicit second-order formula directly; for the order-two terms you get the Hessian matrix, the second-order derivatives. But if you forget this formula, no problem — use the general formula for $k = 2$; it gives the same result, just with more partial derivatives to compute.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open, $k \in \mathbb{N}$, and let $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be $k$ times continuously differentiable. Then for every $x_0 \in U$,

$$f(x) = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} \frac{\partial^\alpha f(x_0)}{\alpha!} (x - x_0)^\alpha + o(\|x - x_0\|^k) \quad (x \rightarrow x_0).$$

The Taylor polynomial,

$$x \mapsto \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} \frac{\partial^\alpha f(x_0)}{\alpha!} (x - x_0)^\alpha,$$

provides a polynomial approximation of $f$ near $x_0$.

*Remark 7.2.2* (Local approximation). /In data analysis, Taylor expansions are used to approximate complex models locally by simpler ones./
#+end_quote

* Theorem: Second-order sufficient conditions for extrema
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:second-order-sufficient-extrema
:SOURCE: ch_05 §5.2.5
:LECTURE: 01
:END:

#+begin_quote
Then we have the analog of what we know from one-dimensional extrema: if the first derivative is $0$ and the second-order term is positive, I have a local minimum. "Second-order positive" means the Hessian matrix is positive definite. You can check the definition directly, or check the eigenvalues: if all eigenvalues are positive the matrix is positive definite; if all are negative it is negative definite. You are allowed to use everything you know from linear algebra here.

Then we have the standard second-order conditions for the constrained case as well. The first-order condition for a constrained extremum is that the gradient of the Lagrangian is $0$ — another way of saying $\nabla f$ is in the normal space. The second-order condition: the Hessian of the Lagrangian with respect to $x$ is positive definite, but only on the tangent space. Then I have a local minimum on the constraint. This is the little twist compared to the unconstrained case: there you check positive-definiteness on all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ via the eigenvalues; here the matrix is square, but you only test vectors $v$ in the tangent space. It can happen — and in one of the exercises it does — that the Hessian is positive definite on the tangent space but not on the whole space. For example, in $\mathbb{R}^2$ it was not positive definite, but on the one-dimensional tangent space it was. So the eigenvalue shortcut no longer works; you really have to use the definition of positive-definiteness.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be twice continuously differentiable and let $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a critical point, i.e. $\operatorname{grad} f(x_0) = 0$.

1. If $H_f(x_0)$ is positive definite, then $x_0$ is a strict local minimum.

2. If $H_f(x_0)$ is negative definite, then $x_0$ is a strict local maximum.

3. If $H_f(x_0)$ is indefinite, then $x_0$ is a saddle point.

*Remark 7.2.6.* /If the Hessian $H_f(x_0)$ is positive semidefinite or negative semidefinite but not definite, the second-order test is inconclusive./

*Remark 7.2.7* (Local extrema). /In data-driven models, local extrema correspond to locally optimal parameter values where small changes do not improve the objective function./
#+end_quote

* Theorem: Gradient descent
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:gradient-descent
:SOURCE: ch_05 §5.2.3
:LECTURE: 01
:END:

#+begin_quote
Then we have gradient descent, which in principle is just this iteration. The gradient of a function points in the direction where the function increases maximally, so if I want to decrease it I go in the direction opposite to the gradient. Unfortunately everything is only local, so I go a little bit in that direction, then compute the gradient again and see how much it changes. You take the gradient of $f$ at some point, go in the direction opposite to where the gradient points, and repeat. Under the right circumstances this converges towards a point where the gradient is $0$ — the necessary condition for a local minimum. For a convex function, if the gradient goes to $0$ we really do find the global minimum. There is also a stochastic version of it.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $f : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be convex and differentiable and assume that

$$\| \nabla f(x) \| \le C \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}^d$$

for some $C > 0$. Let $x^*$ be a minimizer of $f$. For an initial $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$, consider gradient descent

$$x_{k+1} := x_k - \eta \, \nabla f(x_k)$$

with step size $\eta > 0$. Define the average iterate

$$\bar{x}_N := \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} x_k.$$

Then for every $N \ge 1$,

$$|f(\bar{x}_N) - f(x^*)| \le \frac{\| x_0 - x^* \|^2}{2 \eta N} + \frac{\eta C^2}{2}.$$

In particular, with $\eta = \| x_0 - x^* \| / (C \sqrt{N})$,

$$|f(\bar{x}_N) - f(x^*)| \le N^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, C \| x_0 - x^* \|.$$

Thus, the error is bounded by $|f(\bar{x}_N) - f(x^*)|$ decays as $N^{-1/2}$.
#+end_quote

* Definition: Submanifolds
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:submanifold
:SOURCE: ch_05 §5.4.1
:LECTURE: 01
:END:

#+begin_quote
The last chapter was about local extrema on submanifolds, and we introduced submanifolds. If you open an analysis textbook and look for submanifolds, you will find them, but they start in a slightly different way — often one definition is given and then proven equivalent to another. I picked right away what we really need in order to compute the constrained extrema, so this definition deviates a little from some textbooks. Essentially, a submanifold is something given locally as the zero set of a function $g$. To keep it from looking too strange, we require that the derivative of $g$ always has the same rank $r$. In that case the dimension of the manifold is $k$, with $r = n - k$, where $n$ is the dimension of the codomain of $g$. If you do not have this rank condition you get strange sets, but we do not consider those — for us all submanifolds look beautiful, like a circle, with no sharp boundaries, everything smooth.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
A subset $M \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is called a $k$ dimensional submanifold if for every $x_0 \in M$, there exist

- an open neighborhood $U \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ of $x_0$,

- a continuously differentiable function $g : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^r$ with $r = n - k$,

such that

$$M \cap U = \{ x \in U : g(x) = 0 \},$$

and

$$\text{rank}\, g'(x) = r \qquad \forall x \in M \cap U.$$
#+end_quote

Material context (from notes):
Let $M \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be a $k$ -dimensional submanifold and let $x \in M$.

1. tangent space: the tangent space of $M$ at $x$ is

   $$T_x(M) = \{\gamma'(0) : \gamma : (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \rightarrow M \text{ continuously differentiable}, \gamma(0) = x\}.$$

2. normal space: the normal space of $M$ at $x$ is

   $$N_x(M) = T_x(M)^\perp.$$
#+end_quote

Material context (from notes):
Let $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and $f : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. Suppose $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a $k$ -dimensional submanifold. We say that $x \in M \cap \Omega$ is a local maximum of $f$ on $M$ if there is an open neighborhood $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ of $x$ such that

$$f(y) \le f(x), \quad \forall y \in U \cap M \cap \Omega.$$
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $M \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be a $k$ -dimensional submanifold and $x \in M$ with a neighborhood $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ of $x$ such that $g : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^r$ with $r = n - k$ is continuously differentiable with $M \cap U = \{y \in U : g(y) = 0\}$ and $\operatorname{rank} g'(y) = r$ for all $y \in M \cap U$. Then

\begin{align*}
T_x(M) &= \{\nabla g_1(x), \ldots, \nabla g_r(x)\}^\perp, \\
N_x(M) &= \operatorname{span}\{\nabla g_1(x), \ldots, \nabla g_r(x)\}.
\end{align*}

Since the Jacobian of $g$ is

$$g'(x) = \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{grad} g_1(x) \\ \vdots \\ \operatorname{grad} g_r(x) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} (\nabla g_1(x))^\top \\ \vdots \\ (\nabla g_r(x))^\top \end{pmatrix},$$

the rank requirement ensures that $\{\nabla g_1(x), \ldots, \nabla g_r(x)\}$ are linearly independent. The theorem further says that $T_x(M) = \operatorname{null} g'(x) = \{v \in \mathbb{R}^n : g'(x) v = 0\}$ is a linear subspace of dimension $k$, $N_x(M)$ is a $n - k$ -dimensional linear subspace, and

$$\mathbb{R}^n = T_x(M) \oplus N_x(M).$$
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Since $\nabla f$ is in the normal space, it must be a linear combination of the basis vectors of the normal space, so $\nabla f$ is a linear combination, with coefficients $\lambda_i$, of the gradients of the $g_i$. This is where we use these equations to compute the point $x$ from the system. Then we try to get a numerical method out of it, so we do not have to do it by hand every time.

We define a function from $\mathbb{R}^{n+k}$ to the same space, whose first component is the gradient of $f$ minus the sum of $\lambda_i$ times the gradients of the $g_i$, and whose second component is just $g$. Having a local extremum means this capital $F$ is $0$: the first part gives that $\nabla f$ is a linear combination of the $\nabla g_i$, and the second part gives $g(x) = 0$, so $x$ lies on $M$. We are then at a point where at least the necessary conditions are satisfied. I have a function from a space to the same space, I am looking for a zero — this is made for Newton. We implemented this and saw in the exercise classes that it really works.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Material context (from notes):
Let $U \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and let

$$M = \{x \in U : g_1(x) = \cdots = g_r(x) = 0\}$$

be a $k$ -dimensional submanifold. Let

$$f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$$

be continuously differentiable and suppose that $f|_M$ has a local extremum at $x_0 \in M$. Then there are coefficients $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_r \in \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$\nabla f(x_0) = \sum_{i=1}^r \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x_0).$$
#+end_quote
