* Definition:  Leibniz Formula of the determinant
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:leibniz-determinant
:LECTURE_REF: 4.1
:END:

For any $A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$, its determinant is defined as


\begin{align*}
\det(A) = \begin{vmatrix} a_{1,1} & \ldots & a_{1,n} \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ a_{n,1} & \ldots & a_{n,n} \end{vmatrix} &:= \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) a_{1,\sigma(1)} a_{2,\sigma(2)} \ldots a_{n,\sigma(n)} \\
&= \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,\sigma(i)} \\
\end{align*}

** Questions

*** DONE What are $\sigma$ and $\mathcal{S}$?

$\mathcal{S}_n$ is the set of all permutations of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$.


$\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n$ is just an element of a set.

*** DONE What are $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon(\sigma)$?

$\varepsilon$ is the sign function such that:

\begin{align*}
\varepsilon(\sigma) =
\begin{cases}
+1 & \text{if } \varepsilon \text{ is the product of even number of transpositions} \\
-1 & \text{if odd}
\end{cases}
\end{align*}

* Lemma:       Laplace expansion
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:laplace-expansion
:LECTURE_REF: 4.2
:END:

The determinant of a matrix $A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$ is given by

1. If $n = 1$, then $\det(A) = a_{1,1}$.

2. If $n > 1$, then for every $j = 1, \ldots, n$,

   $$\det(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (-1)^{i+j} a_{i,j} \det(A_{i,j}^{\star}).$$

#+begin_proof
We omit it for brevity.
#+end_proof

$$\det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,i}.$$

In particular,

$$\det(\mathrm{Id}_n) = 1.$$

* Lemma:       Determinant of the transpose
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:determinant-transpose
:LECTURE_REF: 4.5
:END:

$$\det(A^\top) = \det(A).$$

#+begin_proof
By the Leibniz formula,

$$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,\sigma(i)},$$

and

$$\det(A^\top) = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} (A^\top)_{i,\sigma(i)} = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{\sigma(i),i}.$$

Now let $\tau = \sigma^{-1}$. Since the map $\sigma \mapsto \sigma^{-1}$ is a bijection on $\mathcal{S}_n$, and since $\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1}) = \varepsilon(\sigma)$, we rewrite the sum as

$$\det(A^\top) = \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\tau) \prod_{j=1}^{n} a_{j,\tau(j)}.$$

Indeed, if $j = \sigma(i)$, then $i = \sigma^{-1}(j) = \tau(j)$, so

$$\prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{\sigma(i),i} = \prod_{j=1}^{n} a_{j,\tau(j)}.$$

Therefore,

$$\det(A^\top) = \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\tau) \prod_{j=1}^{n} a_{j,\tau(j)} = \det(A).$$
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Linearity in each column, vanishing on repeated columns
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:determinant-multilinearity
:LECTURE_REF: 4.7
:END:

1. Linearity with respect to each column: Let $j_0 \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, and let $v, w \in \mathbb{K}^n$ and $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$. Then

   $$\det(u_1, \ldots, u_{j_0-1}, (\lambda v + \mu w), u_{j_0+1}, \ldots, u_n) = \lambda \det(u_1, \ldots, u_{j_0-1}, v, u_{j_0+1}, \ldots, u_n) + \mu \det(u_1, \ldots, u_{j_0-1}, w, u_{j_0+1}, \ldots, u_n). \quad (4.2)$$

2. If two columns are identical, then the determinant is zero: If $u_i = u_j$ for two indices $1 \le i \ne j \le n$, then

   $$\det(u_1, \ldots, u_n) = 0.$$

3. The above two claims also hold for rows instead of columns.

#+begin_proof
(i) Let the matrix $A, B, C \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$ be defined as

$$\begin{align*} A &= (u_1, \ldots, u_{j_0-1}, (\lambda v + \mu w), u_{j_0+1}, \ldots, u_n) \\ B &= (u_1, \ldots, u_{j_0-1}, v, u_{j_0+1}, \ldots, u_n) \\ C &= (u_1, \ldots, u_{j_0-1}, w, u_{j_0+1}, \ldots, u_n) \end{align*}$$

The determinant of $A$ is

$$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) a_{1,\sigma(1)} a_{2,\sigma(2)} \ldots a_{n,\sigma(n)}.$$

Fix $\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n$ and consider the product $a_{1,\sigma(1)} a_{2,\sigma(2)} \ldots a_{n,\sigma(n)}$. In this product, exactly one term comes from the column $j_0$ because there is a unique $i_0$ such that $\sigma(i_0) = j_0$. Since $a_{i_0,\sigma(i_0)} = a_{i_0,j_0} = \lambda v_{i_0} + \mu w_{i_0}$, it follows that

$$\begin{align*} a_{1,\sigma(1)} a_{2,\sigma(2)} \ldots a_{n,\sigma(n)} &= \lambda a_{1,\sigma(1)} \cdots a_{i_0-1,\sigma(i_0-1)} v_{i_0,j_0} a_{i_0+1,\sigma(i_0+1)} \cdots a_{n,\sigma(n)} \\ &\quad + \mu a_{1,\sigma(1)} \cdots a_{i_0-1,\sigma(i_0-1)} w_{i_0,j_0} a_{i_0+1,\sigma(i_0+1)} \cdots a_{n,\sigma(n)} \\ &= \lambda b_{1,\sigma(1)} \cdots b_{i_0-1,\sigma(i_0-1)} b_{i_0,j_0} b_{i_0+1,\sigma(i_0+1)} \cdots b_{n,\sigma(n)} \\ &\quad + \mu c_{1,\sigma(1)} \cdots c_{i_0-1,\sigma(i_0-1)} c_{i_0,j_0} c_{i_0+1,\sigma(i_0+1)} \cdots c_{n,\sigma(n)}. \end{align*}$$

We now multiply this last expression by $\varepsilon(\sigma)$ and sum over $\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n$. This leads to Eq. (4.2).

(ii) Let $A = (u_1, \ldots, u_n)$ with $u_i = u_j = v$ for two indices $1 \le i \ne j \le n$. Fix $\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n$, and consider the product $a_{1,\sigma(1)} a_{2,\sigma(2)} \ldots a_{n,\sigma(n)}$. There exist indices $i_0, j_0 \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$ such that $\sigma(i_0) = i$ and $\sigma(j_0) = j$. For that fixed $\sigma$, there exists $\tilde{\sigma}$ such that $\tilde{\sigma}(i_0) = j$ and $\tilde{\sigma}(j_0) = i$ and $\sigma(k) = \tilde{\sigma}(k)$ for any other index $k \ne i_0, j_0$. These permutations $\sigma$ and $\tilde{\sigma}$ differ only by one swap, therefore $\varepsilon(\sigma) = -\varepsilon(\tilde{\sigma})$. Consequently,

$$\begin{align*} \varepsilon(\sigma) a_{1,\sigma(1)} a_{2,\sigma(2)} & \ldots a_{n,\sigma(n)} + \varepsilon(\tilde{\sigma}) a_{1,\tilde{\sigma}(1)} a_{2,\tilde{\sigma}(2)} \ldots a_{n,\tilde{\sigma}(n)} \\ &= \varepsilon(\sigma) (a_{i_0,\sigma(i_0)} a_{j_0,\sigma(j_0)} - a_{i_0,\tilde{\sigma}(i_0)} a_{j_0,\tilde{\sigma}(j_0)}) \prod_{k \ne i_0, j_0} a_{k,\sigma(k)} \\ &= \varepsilon(\sigma) (a_{i_0,i} a_{j_0,j} - a_{i_0,j} a_{j_0,i}) \prod_{k \ne i_0, j_0} a_{k,\sigma(k)} \\ &= 0 \quad \text{(since columns } i \text{ and } j \text{ are identical)}. \end{align*}$$

Summing over all permutations leads to the conclusion that $\det(A) = 0$.

(iii) Since $\det(A) = \det(A^\top)$, the claims also apply for rows.
#+end_proof

* Corollary:   Elementary operations and the determinant
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: cor:elementary-operations-determinant
:LECTURE_REF: 4.8
:END:

1. Adding a multiple of a column or a row to another: If $i$ and $j$ are two integers in $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ with $j \ne i$, and if $\alpha$ is any scalar, then

   $$\det(u_1, \ldots, u_{j-1}, (u_j + \alpha u_i), u_{j+1}, \ldots, u_n) = \det(u_1, \ldots, u_n).$$

   In other words, an operation of the form

   $$C_j \leftarrow C_j + \alpha C_i \quad (i \ne j)$$

   does not change the determinant. This also holds for operations per rows: or

   $$R_j \leftarrow R_j + \alpha R_i \quad (i \ne j)$$

   does not change the determinant.

2. Scaling a single column/row: It holds that

   $$\det(u_1, \ldots, u_{j-1}, \alpha u_j, u_{j+1}, \ldots, u_n) = \alpha \det(u_1, \ldots, u_{j-1}, u_j, u_{j+1}, \ldots, u_n)$$

   and the same holds for rows.

3. Scaling the whole matrix:

   $$\det(\lambda A) = \lambda^n \det(A).$$

4. Swapping two columns or two rows: If $n \ge 2$, if $u_1, \ldots, u_n$ are vectors of $\mathbb{K}^n$, and if $i < j$ are two distinct integers between $1$ and $n$, then

   $$\det(u_1, \ldots, u_{i-1}, u_j, u_{i+1}, \ldots, u_{j-1}, u_i, u_{j+1}, \ldots, u_n) = (-1) \det(u_1, \ldots, u_n).$$

   So when one swaps two rows of a matrix $A$, the determinant is replaced by its opposite.

5. The determinant of linearly dependent vectors is zero: If $u_1, \ldots, u_n \in \mathbb{K}^n$ is a linearly dependent set, then $\det(u_1, \ldots, u_n) = 0$.

#+begin_proof
These are direct consequences of [[#lem:determinant-multilinearity][Lemma 4.7]]. Left as an exercise.
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       We can compute the determinant by the pivot method
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:determinant-pivot
:LECTURE_REF: 4.10
:END:

We will give the statement in a narrated way, and in a more mathematical way:

- By a sequence of operations of types (a) to (c) above, one can reduce the computation of the determinant of any matrix $A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$ to that of an upper triangular matrix.

- It holds that $\det(A) = (-1)^s \prod_{i=1}^{n} u_{i,i}$ where $U$ is the upper triangular matrix from the $PLU$ decomposition of $A$ and $s$ is the number of row swaps in the permutation matrix $P$.

#+begin_proof
$$\begin{align*} \det(A) &= \det(PLU) \\ &= \det(P) \det(L) \det(U) \quad \text{(by [[#lem:determinant-product][Lemma 4.12]] stated just below)} \\ &= (-1)^s \prod_{i=1}^{n} u_{i,i}, \end{align*}$$

where in the last line we have used that $\det(P) = (-1)^s$, that $L$ is a lower triangular matrix with unity diagonal (hence $\det(L) = 1$), and that $U$ is a triangular matrix.
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Determinant of the matrix product
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:determinant-product
:LECTURE_REF: 4.12
:END:

Let $A, B \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$. It holds that

$$\det(AB) = \det(A) \det(B)$$

#+begin_proof
Let $C = AB = (c_{ij})_{1 \le i,j \le n}$, where $c_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}$. The Leibniz formula for the determinant of $C$ reads

$$\det(C) = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} c_{i,\sigma(i)} = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} \left( \sum_{k_i=1}^{n} a_{i k_i} b_{k_i, \sigma(i)} \right).$$

Expanding the product of sums gives

$$\det(AB) = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \sum_{k_1, \ldots, k_n = 1}^{n} \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i k_i} b_{k_i, \sigma(i)}.$$

Reordering the sums,

$$\det(AB) = \sum_{k_1, \ldots, k_n = 1}^{n} \left( \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i k_i} \right) \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} b_{k_i, \sigma(i)}.$$

We now observe that if two indices $k_r = k_s$ with $r \ne s$, then two rows in the matrix $(b_{k_i, j})$ are equal, so

$$\sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} b_{k_i, \sigma(i)} = 0.$$

Hence only tuples $(k_1, \ldots, k_n)$ with distinct entries contribute. Since each $k_i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, such a tuple must be a permutation $\tau \in \mathcal{S}_n$, i.e.

$$k_i = \tau(i).$$

Therefore,

$$\det(AB) = \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{S}_n} \left( \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i, \tau(i)} \right) \sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} b_{\tau(i), \sigma(i)}.$$

Now reindex the inner product by $j = \tau(i)$. Then

$$\prod_{i=1}^{n} b_{\tau(i), \sigma(i)} = \prod_{j=1}^{n} b_{j, (\sigma \circ \tau^{-1})(j)}.$$

Thus,

$$\sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} b_{\tau(i), \sigma(i)} = \varepsilon(\tau) \sum_{\pi \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\pi) \prod_{j=1}^{n} b_{j, \pi(j)},$$

where $\pi = \sigma \circ \tau^{-1}$. Hence,

$$\sum_{\sigma \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} b_{\tau(i), \sigma(i)} = \varepsilon(\tau) \det(B).$$

Substituting back,

$$\det(AB) = \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{S}_n} \left( \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i, \tau(i)} \right) \varepsilon(\tau) \det(B).$$

Factoring out $\det(B)$,

$$\det(AB) = \det(B) \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{S}_n} \varepsilon(\tau) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i, \tau(i)}.$$

The remaining sum is exactly $\det(A)$. Therefore $\det(AB) = \det(A) \det(B)$.
#+end_proof

* Corollary:   Determinant and invertibility
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: cor:determinant-invertibility
:LECTURE_REF: 4.13
:END:

A matrix $A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$ is invertible if and only if

$$\det(A) \ne 0.$$

In that case,

$$\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}.$$

#+begin_proof
- If $A$ is invertible, then

  $$A(A^{-1}) = I_n,$$

  and therefore

  $$\det(A) \det(A^{-1}) = \det(I_n) = 1,$$

  so $\det(A)$ cannot be zero, and indeed

  $$\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}.$$

- If $A$ is not invertible, then there exists a linear dependence relation among its rows, and we have already seen that in this case the determinant of $A$ is zero.
#+end_proof

* Corollary:   Similar matrices have the same determinant
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: cor:similar-matrices-determinant
:LECTURE_REF: 4.15
:END:

If $A$ and $B$ are two similar matrices, then

$$\det(A) = \det(B).$$

#+begin_proof
To say that $A$ and $B$ are similar means that there exists an invertible matrix $P$ such that

$$A = PBP^{-1}.$$

Hence

$$\det(A) = \det(P) \det(B) \det(P^{-1}).$$

But we have just seen that

$$\det(P^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(P)},$$

and the product

$$\det(P) \det(B) \frac{1}{\det(P)}$$

is just a product of real numbers, so it is of course equal to $\det(B)$.
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Determinant of an endomorphism
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:endomorphism-determinant
:LECTURE_REF: 4.16

Let $E$ be an $n$ -dimensional $\mathbb{K}$ vector space and let $f : E \rightarrow E$ be an endomorphism of $E$ ($f \in \mathcal{L}(E)$). We define the determinant of $f$ as the determinant of its matrix representation $[f]_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{E}}$ on a basis $\mathcal{E}$ of $E$. The determinant is independent to the choice of the basis,

$$\det(f) := \det([f]_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{E}}), \quad \forall \mathcal{E} \text{ basis of } E.$$

* Definition:  Alternating $n$ -linear form
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:alternating-multilinear-form
:LECTURE_REF: 4.17
:END:

Let $E$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{K}$, let $n$ be a positive integer, and let

$$\Phi : E^n \rightarrow \mathbb{K}, \qquad (u_1, \ldots, u_n) \longmapsto \Phi(u_1, \ldots, u_n)$$

be a map which, starting from $n$ vectors in $E$, produces a scalar.

1. We say that $\Phi$ is a /n-linear form/ if it is linear in each of its variables: whenever one fixes an $i \in \{1, \ldots, k\}$ and vectors

   $$u_1, \ldots, u_{i-1}, u_{i+1}, \ldots, u_n \in E,$$

   the map

   $$E \rightarrow \mathbb{K}, \qquad v \longmapsto \Phi(u_1, \ldots, u_{i-1}, v, u_{i+1}, \ldots, u_n)$$

   is linear.

2. We say that $\Phi$ is /alternating/ if, whenever two of the vectors among $(u_1, \ldots, u_n)$ are identical, then

   $$\Phi(u_1, \ldots, u_n) = 0.$$

** Questions

* Corollary:   Determinant is an alternating $n$ -linear form
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: cor:determinant-alternating-form
:LECTURE_REF: 4.18
:END:

The map

$$(u_1, \ldots, u_n) \longmapsto \det(u_1, \ldots, u_n)$$

is an alternating $n$ -linear form on the space $E = \mathbb{K}^n$.

It is easy to check that if $\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$ are two alternating $n$ -linear forms on a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space $E$, and if $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are two scalars in $\mathbb{K}$, then the map

$$\lambda_1 \Phi_1 + \lambda_2 \Phi_2$$

is an alternating $n$ -linear form. Thus the set of alternating $n$ -linear forms on $E$ also contains the zero map. It is therefore a vector subspace of $\mathcal{F}(E^n, \mathbb{K})$, and it is traditionally denoted by $\Lambda^n(E)$.

The following theorem may appear abstract, but the most concrete properties of the determinant that we have proven earlier follow from it. We will not detail its proof.

** Questions

* Theorem:     All alternating $n$ -linear forms on an $n$ -dimensional space are proportional
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:alternating-forms-proportional
:LECTURE_REF: 4.19
:END:

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional vector space, and let $n$ denote the dimension of $E$. Then the space $\Lambda^n(E)$ has dimension 1. Consequently, if $\Phi$ is an alternating $n$ -linear form on $\mathbb{K}^n$, then there exists a scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{K}$ such that

$$\Phi = \lambda \det$$

As an example of application, we next prove [[#lem:determinant-product][Lemma 4.12]] about $\det(AB) = \det(A) \det(B)$ using the more axiomatic properties of multilinear forms.

#+begin_proof
Alternative proof of [[#lem:determinant-product][Lemma 4.12]]. Let $A, B \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$, and write the columns of $B$ as

$$B = (b_1 \mid b_2 \mid \cdots \mid b_n),$$

where $b_1, \ldots, b_n \in \mathbb{K}^n$. Then the columns of $AB$ are $Ab_1, \ldots, Ab_n$, so

$$AB = (Ab_1 \mid Ab_2 \mid \cdots \mid Ab_n).$$

Define a map

$$\omega : (\mathbb{K}^n)^n \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$$

by

$$\omega(v_1, \ldots, v_n) = \det(Av_1, \ldots, Av_n).$$

Since the map $v \mapsto Av$ is linear and the determinant is $n$ -linear in the columns, the map $\omega$ is $n$ -linear. Moreover, if $v_i = v_j$ for some $i \ne j$, then

$$Av_i = Av_j,$$

so the matrix $(Av_1, \ldots, Av_n)$ has two equal columns. Hence,

$$\omega(v_1, \ldots, v_n) = 0.$$

Therefore, $\omega$ is an alternating $n$ -linear form on $\mathbb{K}^n$. By the [[#thm:alternating-forms-proportional][Theorem 4.19]] for alternating $n$ -linear forms on $E = \mathbb{K}^n$, there exists a scalar $c \in \mathbb{K}$ such that

$$\omega(v_1, \ldots, v_n) = \lambda \det(v_1, \ldots, v_n)$$

for all $v_1, \ldots, v_n \in \mathbb{K}^n$. To determine $\lambda$, evaluate at the standard basis vectors $e_1, \ldots, e_n$:

$$\lambda = \lambda \det(e_1, \ldots, e_n) = \omega(e_1, \ldots, e_n).$$

Since

$$\det(e_1, \ldots, e_n) = 1,$$

we obtain

$$\lambda = \omega(e_1, \ldots, e_n) = \det(Ae_1, \ldots, Ae_n).$$

But $Ae_1, \ldots, Ae_n$ are exactly the columns of $A$. Therefore,

$$\lambda = \det(A).$$

Hence, for all $v_1, \ldots, v_n \in \mathbb{K}^n$,

$$\det(Av_1, \ldots, Av_n) = \det(A) \det(v_1, \ldots, v_n).$$

Now apply this to the columns $b_1, \ldots, b_n$ of $B$:

$$\det(AB) = \det(Ab_1, \ldots, Ab_n) = \det(A) \det(b_1, \ldots, b_n) = \det(A) \det(B).$$
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Volume of a parallelotope
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:volume-parallelotope
:LECTURE_REF: 4.20
:END:

Let $E$ be an $n$ dimensional inner-product vector space and let $\mathcal{E}$ be an orthonormal basis. The volume of the parallelotope associated to a set of $n$ vectors $u_1, \ldots, u_n \in E$ is defined as

$$\text{Vol}(\mathcal{P}(u_1, \ldots, u_n)) := |\det([u_1]^{\mathcal{E}}, \ldots, [u_n]^{\mathcal{E}})|.$$

For any non-orthonormal basis $\mathcal{F}$, it holds that

$$\text{Vol}(\mathcal{P}(u_1, \ldots, u_n)) = \left|\det([\text{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}})\right| \left|\det([u_1]^{\mathcal{F}}, \ldots, [u_n]^{\mathcal{F}})\right|.$$

#+begin_proof
To prove Eq. (4.4), it suffices to recall that, by Eq. (1.6), $[u_i]^{\mathcal{E}} = [\text{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}} [u_i]^{\mathcal{F}}$. Thus

\begin{align*}
\text{Vol}(\mathcal{P}(u_1, \ldots, u_n)) &= |\det([u_1]^{\mathcal{E}}, \ldots, [u_n]^{\mathcal{E}})| \\
&= \left|\det([\text{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}} [u_1]^{\mathcal{F}}, \ldots, [\text{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}} [u_n]^{\mathcal{F}})\right| \\
&= \left|\det([\text{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}} ([u_1]^{\mathcal{F}} | \ldots | [u_n]^{\mathcal{F}}))\right| \\
&= \left|\det([\text{Id}]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{F}})\right| \left|\det([u_1]^{\mathcal{F}}, \ldots, [u_n]^{\mathcal{F}})\right|.
\end{align*}
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Volume scaling by endomorphism
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:volume-scaling-endomorphism
:LECTURE_REF: 4.21
:END:

Let $E$ be an $n$ dimensional inner-product space, let $f : E \rightarrow E$ be an endomorphism, and let $u_1, \ldots, u_n$ be a set of vectors in $E$. It holds that for any orthonormal basis $\mathcal{E}$ of $E$,

$$\text{Vol}(\mathcal{P}(f(u_1), \ldots, f(u_n))) = \left|\det([f]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}})\right| \text{Vol}(\mathcal{P}(u_1, \ldots, u_n))$$

#+begin_proof
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be an orthonormal basis of $E$. We have that

\begin{align*}
\text{Vol}(\mathcal{P}(f(u_1), \ldots, f(u_n))) &= \left|\det([f(u_1)]^{\mathcal{E}}, \ldots, [f(u_n)]^{\mathcal{E}})\right| \\
&= \left|\det([f]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}} \left([u_1]^{\mathcal{E}}, \ldots, [u_n]^{\mathcal{E}}\right))\right| \\
&= \left|\det([f]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}})\right| \left|\det\left(([u_1]^{\mathcal{E}}, \ldots, [u_n]^{\mathcal{E}})\right)\right| \\
&= \left|\det([f]^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{E}})\right| \text{Vol}(\mathcal{P}(u_1, \ldots, u_n)).
\end{align*}
#+end_proof

* Theorem:     Hadamard's inequality
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: thm:hadamard-inequality
:LECTURE_REF: 4.22
:END:

Let $A = (a_1 | \ldots | a_n) \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$, then

$$|\det(A)| \le \prod_{i=1}^{n} \|a_i\|_2,$$

and equality holds if and only if the columns are pairwise orthogonal, that is,

$$\langle a_i, a_j \rangle_2 = 0, \quad \text{for } 1 \le i \ne j \le n.$$

The result also holds by rows instead of columns.

#+begin_proof
The proof requires eigenvalue theory which we present in the next chapter. Let $A = (a_1 | \ldots | a_n) \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$, and define $B \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$ as the matrix $A$ with normalized columns,

$$B := \left(\frac{a_1}{\|a_1\|_1} | \ldots | \frac{a_n}{\|a_n\|_2}\right).$$

We next consider the matrix $P = B^H B = (\langle b_i, b_j \rangle_2)_{1 \le i,j \le n}$. The matrix $P$ is Hermitian and positive definite, therefore diagonalizable and with real, nonnegative eigenvalues. Let $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n \in \mathbb{R}_+$ be the eigenvalues. Using the inequality of arithmetic-geometric means,

$$\det(P) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i \le \left(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i\right)^n = \left(\frac{1}{n} \text{Tr}(P)\right)^n = \left(\frac{1}{n} n\right)^n = 1.$$

Finally, since

$$\det(P) = \det(B)^2 = \frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{n} \|a_i\|_2^2} \det(A)^2$$

we infer that $|\det(A)| \le \prod_{i=1}^{n} \|a_i\|_2$ which is the desired inequality.

We finish the proof by showing that

$$|\det(A)| = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \|a_i\|_2 \iff \langle a_i, a_j \rangle_2 = 0, \quad \text{for } 1 \le i \ne j \le n.$$

The direction $\Leftarrow$ follows very directly: If $\langle a_i, a_j \rangle_2 = 0$ for all $i \ne j$, then $P = \text{Id}$ and equality readily follows. To show the direction $\Rightarrow$, we observe that in this case it holds that the eigenvalues of $P$ are such that

$$\prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i = \left(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i\right)^n = 1$$
#+end_proof

* Proposition: Cramer's rule
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: prop:cramers-rule
:LECTURE_REF: 4.23
:END:

Let $A = (a_1 | \ldots | a_n) \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$ be an invertible matrix with columns $a_i \in \mathbb{K}^n$, and let $b \in \mathbb{K}^n$. The solution to the linear system $Ax = b$ is the vector $x = (x_i)_{i=1}^{n}$ where

$$x_i = \frac{\det(a_1 | \ldots | a_{i-1} | b | a_{i+1} | \ldots | a_n)}{\det A}, \quad 1 \le i \le n.$$

#+begin_proof
Since the determinant is an alternating multilinear form, it holds that

$$\det\left(a_1 | \ldots | a_{i-1} | \lambda_i a_i + \sum_{j \ne i} \lambda_j a_j | a_{i+1} | \ldots | a_n\right) = \lambda_i \det A,$$

for all $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$ in $\mathbb{K}$. Since $Ax = b$ is equivalent to writing $b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i a_i$, we can take $\lambda_i = x_i$ in the previous formula and obtain

$$\det(a_1 | \ldots | a_{i-1} | b | a_{i+1} | \ldots | a_n) = x_i \det A,$$

which proves the formula.
#+end_proof
