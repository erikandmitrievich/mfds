* Problem 1 :DONE:
** Task
Verify the following trigonometric identities and check them in Julia.
$\cos(-x)=\cos(x)$
$\sin(-x)=-\sin(x)$
$\cos(x)=\sin(x+\frac{\pi}{2})$
$\sin(x)=\cos(x-\frac{\pi}{2})$
$\sin(x+\pi)=-\sin(x)$
$\cos(x+\pi)=-\cos(x)$,

** Solution
*** $\cos(-x)=\cos(x)$
#+begin_quote
Tailor Series Expansion
$$
cos(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!} \qquad
sin(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} \qquad
$$
#+end_quote

Lets assume $cos(-x) = cos(x)$. Hence,

$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k (-x)^{2k}}{(2k)!} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!}$

Since $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$, $(-x)^{2k} = x^{2k}$. Thus,

$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!}$

$cos(-x) = cos(x)$

*** $\sin(-x)=-\sin(x)$
#+begin_quote
Tailor Series Expansion
$$
cos(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!} \qquad
sin(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} \qquad
$$
#+end_quote

$sin(-x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k (-x)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$

Expression $2k+1$, where $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$ would always be odd.

$sin(-x) = -\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{(-1)^k (x)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$

$sin(-x) = -sin(x)$

*** $\cos(x)=\sin(x+\frac{\pi}{2})$
#+begin_quote
Trigonometric identity:
$$\sin(A + B) = \sin(A)\cos(B) + \cos(A)\sin(B)$$
#+end_quote

$sin(x + \dfrac{\pi}{2}) = sin(x)cos(\dfrac{\pi}{2}) + cos(x)sin(\dfrac{\pi}{2})$

$sin(x + \dfrac{\pi}{2}) = cos(x)$

*** $\sin(x)=\cos(x-\frac{\pi}{2})$
#+begin_quote
Trigonometric identity:
$$\cos(A - B) = cos(A)cos(B) + sin(A)sin(B)$$
#+end_quote

$\cos(x - \dfrac{\pi}{2}) = cos(x)cos(\dfrac{\pi}{2}) + sin(x)sin(\dfrac{\pi}{2})$

$\cos(x - \dfrac{\pi}{2}) = sin(x)$

*** $\sin(x+\pi)=-\sin(x)$
#+begin_quote
Trigonometric identity:
$$\sin(A + B) = \sin(A)\cos(B) + \cos(A)\sin(B)$$
#+end_quote

$\sin(x + \pi) = \sin(x)\cos(\pi) + \cos(x)\sin(\pi)$

$\sin(x + \pi) = -\sin(x)$

*** $\cos(x+\pi)=-\cos(x)$
#+begin_quote
Trigonometric identity:
$$\cos(A + B) = cos(A)cos(B) - sin(A)sin(B)$$
#+end_quote

$\cos(x + \pi) = cos(x)cos(\pi) - sin(x)sin(\pi)$

$\cos(x + \pi) = -cos(x)$

* Problem 3 :DONE:
** Task
Suppose that $f,g:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ are continuous in $x_{0}\in U$. Prove that if $f$ is also differentiable in $x_{0}$ and $f(x_{0})=0$ then the product $f g$ is differentiable in $x_{0}$.
Illustrate this claim in Julia for the functions $f(x)=x$ and $g(x)=|x|$.

** Solution

#+begin_quote
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be an open interval and $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. We say that $f$ is differentiable at $x \in U$ if the limit

$$\lim_{y \rightarrow x} \dfrac{f(y) - f(x)}{y - x}$$

exists. The value of this limit is denoted by $f'(x)$ and is called a derivative of $f$.
#+end_quote

To prove the product $fg$ is differentiable in $x_{0}$, I have to examine the limit of the difference quotient for the product function $(fg)(x)$ at $x_0$.

$$\lim_{x \rightarrow x_{0}} \dfrac{(fg)(x) - (fg)(x_{0})}{x - x_{0}}$$

Given the hypothesis that $f(x_0) = 0$, limit can be simplified.

\begin{align*}
\lim_{x \rightarrow x_{0}} \dfrac{(fg)(x) - (fg)(x_{0})}{x - x_{0}} &= \lim_{x \rightarrow x_{0}} \dfrac{(fg)(x)}{x - x_{0}} \\
&= \lim_{x \rightarrow x_{0}} \dfrac{f(x) - f(x_{0})}{x - x_{0}} g(x) \\
&= \lim_{x \rightarrow x_{0}} \dfrac{f(x) - f(x_{0})}{x - x_{0}} \cdot \lim_{x \rightarrow x_{0}} g(x) \\
&= f'(x_{0}) g(x_{0}) \\
\end{align*}

Since the limit exists, the function product is differentiable at $x_{0}$.

* Problem 5 :DONE:
** Task
Prove by induction (and using the product rule) that, for $1\le n\in\mathbb{N}$ the derivative of $f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$, $f(x)=x^{n}$ is given by $f^{\prime}(x)=nx^{n-1}$.

** Notes
#+begin_quote
*Proposition* The statement S1, S2, S3, S4, ... are all true.

Proof. (Induction)
1. Prove that the first statement S1 is true
2. Given any integer k >= 1, prove that the statement Sk => Sk+1 is true.

It follows by mathematical induction that every Sn is true.
#+end_quote

** Solution
*Proposition.* For $1 \le n \in \mathbb{N} : f : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, f(x) = x^n \implies f'(x) = nx^{n-1}$.

*Proof.*

*Base Case.* $n = 1$.
$f(x) = x^1 = x$.
Using the limit definition:
$f'(x) = \lim_{h\rightarrow0} \dfrac{(x+h) - x}{h} = \lim_{h\rightarrow0} \dfrac{h}{h} = 1$.

Using the formula:
$nx^{n-1} = 1x^{1-1} = 1$.

Base holds.

*Induction Hypothesis.* Assume statement holds for $n = k : \dfrac{d}{dx}(x^k) = kx^{k-1}$.

*Inductive Step.*
We must show that the statement holds for $n = k+1$.
Target: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{k+1}) = (k+1)x^k$.

Rewrite the term:
$x^{k+1} = x \cdot x^k$

Apply the Product Rule ($u=x, v=x^k$):
$\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{k+1}) = \dfrac{d}{dx} \left[ x \cdot x^k \right]$

$\dfrac{d}{dx}(x \cdot x^k) = \left[ \dfrac{d}{dx} x \right] \cdot x^k + x \cdot \left[ \dfrac{d}{dx} x^k \right]$

Substitute derivatives (Base Case and Hypothesis):
$\dfrac{d}{dx}(x \cdot x^k) = (1) \cdot x^k + x \cdot (kx^{k-1})$

Simplify:
$\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{k+1}) = x^k + kx^{1 + k - 1}$
$\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{k+1}) = x^k + kx^k$

Factor:
$\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{k+1}) = (1+k)x^k$
$\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{k+1}) = (k+1)x^k$

*Conclusion.* Since $S_1$ is true and $S_k \implies S_{k+1}$, the proposition is true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ by mathematical induction.

- Part (3)

  \begin{align*}
        x_{n+1} - \sqrt{2} &= \frac{1}{2} (x_{n} + \frac{2}{x_{n}}) - \sqrt{2} \\
        &= \frac{x_{n}}{2} + \frac{1}{x_{n}} - \sqrt{2} \\
        &= \frac{x_{n}^2}{2x_{n}} + \frac{2}{2x_{n}} - \frac{2x_{n}\sqrt{2}}{2x_{n}} \\
        &= \frac{x_{n}^2 + 2 - 2x_{n}\sqrt{2}}{2x_{n}} \\
        &= \frac{x_{n}^2 - 2\sqrt{2}x_{n} + 2}{2x_{n}} \\
        &= \frac{(x_{n} - \sqrt{2})^{2}}{2x_{n}} \\
  \end{align*}

  Since $(x_{n} - \sqrt{2})^{2} \geq 0$ and $2x_{n} > 0$ (as $x_n > 0$), $\frac{(x_{n} - \sqrt{2})^{2}}{2x_{n}} \ge 0$.

- Part (4)

  \begin{align*}
        \frac{x_{n+1}}{x_{n}} &= \frac{\frac{1}{2}(x_{n}+\frac{2}{x_{n}})}{x_{n}} \\
        &= \frac{x_{n}+\frac{2}{x_{n}}}{2x_{n}} \\
        &= \frac{x_{n}^{2}+2}{2x_{n}^{2}} \\
  \end{align*}

  Since $x_{n} \ge \sqrt{2}$ (as proved in part (3)):

  - Case 1: $x_{n} = \sqrt{2}$

    \begin{align*}
        \frac{(\sqrt{2})^{2}+2}{2(\sqrt{2})^{2}} &= \frac{2 + 2}{2 \cdot 2} \\
        &= \frac{4}{4} \\
        &= 1 \\
    \end{align*}

  - Case 2: $x_{n} > \sqrt{2}$

    As for $x_{n} > \sqrt{2}$, $x_{n}^{2} + 2 < x_{n}^{2} + x_{n}^{2}$. Thus,

    $$ \frac{x_{n}^{2}+2}{2x_{n}^{2}} < 1 $$

    Hence, $(x_{n})_{n\ge 1}$ is decreasing.

- Part (5)

  Since $(x_{n})_{n \ge 1}$ is monotone decreasing and bounded from below $(x_{n} \ge \sqrt{2})$, by the Monotone Convergence Theorem, the sequence $(x_{n})_{n \ge 1}$ is convergent and has a limit $L \ge \sqrt{2}$.

  I find the limit by taking a limit of both sides:

  \begin{align*}
        \lim_{x_{n+1} \rightarrow L} x_{n+1} &= \lim_{x_{n} \rightarrow L} \frac{1}{2}(x_{n}+\frac{2}{x_{n}}) \\
        L &= \frac{1}{2}(L+\frac{2}{L}) \\
        2L &= L+\frac{2}{L} \\
        L &= \frac{2}{L} \\
        L^{2} &= 2 \\
        L &= \sqrt{2} \\
  \end{align*}

    global curr_a, curr_b

    push!(b_history, c)
