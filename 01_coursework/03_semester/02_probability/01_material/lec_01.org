* Data and probability models

Observed data and a probability model are different mathematical objects:

\[ \text{data: what was observed} \neq \text{model: how data could arise} \]

A probability model can be useful without being a literal description of reality. A process may even be deterministic, but too complex or insufficiently known to model directly.

This course studies the consequences of specified probability models through proofs and examples. Statistical inference belongs to the separate statistics course; the table below distinguishes the two directions.

| Probability theory                                              | Statistics                                                                 |
|-----------------------------------------------------------------+----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| The model and its probabilities are fixed and treated as known. | Data are observed, while the model is unknown or only partially specified. |
| We derive probabilities, typical behavior, and predictions.     | We use data to estimate, compare, or test plausible models.                |

Schematically,

\[ \text{fixed model} \longrightarrow \text{possible data}, \qquad \text{observed data} \longrightarrow \text{information about the model} \]

* Outcomes, events, and relative frequency

*Definition 1 (Sample space and event).* The /sample space/ $\Omega$ is the nonempty set of all outcomes allowed by the model. For a given sample space $\Omega$, an /event/ is a subset $A \subseteq \Omega$. For a realized outcome $\omega \in \Omega$, the event occurs exactly when $\omega \in A$.

For events $A, B \subseteq \Omega$, complements, unions, and intersections represent logical operations:

\begin{align*}
A^c &= \Omega \setminus A && \text{not } A, \\
A \cup B & && A \text{ or } B, \text{ or both,} \\
A \cap B & && A \text{ and } B, \\
A \setminus B & && A, \text{ but not } B
\end{align*}

The events $A$ and $B$ are /disjoint/ if $A \cap B = \emptyset$. The impossible event is $\emptyset$, and the certain event is $\Omega$.

Let $\Omega$ be a sample space, let $n \in \mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, \dots\}$, let $A \subseteq \Omega$, and let $\omega_i \in \Omega$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$ be the observed outcomes. Define the indicator $\mathbf{1}_A \colon \Omega \to \{0, 1\}$, for $\omega \in \Omega$, and the relative frequency by

\[ \mathbf{1}_A(\omega) = \begin{cases} 1, & \omega \in A, \\ 0, & \omega \notin A, \end{cases} \qquad \widehat{p}_n(A) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}_A(\omega_i) \]

The relative frequency $\widehat{p}_n(A)$ is the fraction of these $n$ observations that lie in $A$. For any sample space $\Omega$, $n \in \mathbb{N}$, events $A, B \subseteq \Omega$, and fixed observations $\omega_i \in \Omega$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, the relative frequencies satisfy

\[ 0 \leq \widehat{p}_n(A) \leq 1, \qquad \widehat{p}_n(\emptyset) = 0, \qquad \widehat{p}_n(\Omega) = 1 \]

and, for disjoint $A, B$,

\[ \widehat{p}_n(A \cup B) = \widehat{p}_n(A) + \widehat{p}_n(B) \]

* Probability spaces

A sigma-algebra is a catalogue of the yes/no questions to which the model assigns probabilities. It is closed under the corresponding logical operations.

*Definition 2 (Sigma-algebra).* Let $\Omega$ be a sample space and let $2^\Omega = \{ A : A \subseteq \Omega \}$ be its /power set/, the collection of all its subsets. A collection $\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega$ is a /sigma-algebra/ on $\Omega$ if:

1. $\emptyset \in \mathcal{F}$;
2. $A \in \mathcal{F}$ implies $A^c \in \mathcal{F}$;
3. $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F}$ implies $\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \in \mathcal{F}$.

The members of $\mathcal{F}$ are called events.

Thus $\Omega$ describes what may happen, while $\mathcal{F}$ describes which distinctions the model makes.

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a sigma-algebra on a sample space $\Omega$. Then $\Omega = \emptyset^c \in \mathcal{F}$. For a sequence of events $A_k \in \mathcal{F}$, $k \in \mathbb{N}$, De Morgan's law gives

\[ \bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k = \left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k^c \right)^c \in \mathcal{F} \]

All complements are relative to $\Omega$. Thus a sigma-algebra is also closed under countable intersections. Exercise 4 asks for a proof of the identity.

*Example 1 (Coarse information).* For a die, let $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ and $A = \{2, 4, 6\}$. Then

\[ \mathcal{F} = \{\emptyset, A, A^c, \Omega\} \]

is a sigma-algebra that records only whether the outcome is even or odd. In contrast, $2^\Omega$ distinguishes all subsets of $\Omega$.

For a finite or countable sample space $\Omega$, we often choose $\mathcal{F} = 2^\Omega$. On $\Omega = \mathbb{R}$, a standard choice is the /Borel sigma-algebra/ $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$, the smallest sigma-algebra containing all open intervals.

*Definition 3 (Measurable space, probability measure, and probability space).* Let $\Omega$ be a sample space and let $\mathcal{F}$ be a sigma-algebra on $\Omega$. The pair $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ is called a /measurable space/. A function $\mathbb{P} \colon \mathcal{F} \to [0, 1]$ is a /probability measure/ if

\[ \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1 \]

and, for every sequence of events $A_k \in \mathcal{F}$, $k \in \mathbb{N}$, that are /pairwise disjoint/, meaning $A_k \cap A_\ell = \emptyset$ whenever $k \neq \ell$,

\[ \mathbb{P}\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \right) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(A_k) \]

The triplet $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ is a /probability space/.

*Proposition 1 (First consequences).* Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and let $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$. Then

\begin{align*}
\mathbb{P}(\emptyset) &= 0, \qquad \mathbb{P}(A^c) = 1 - \mathbb{P}(A), \\
A \subseteq B &\implies \mathbb{P}(A) \leq \mathbb{P}(B), \\
\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) &= \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B), \\
\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) &\leq \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B)
\end{align*}

For a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$, an event $A \in \mathcal{F}$, a sample size $n \in \mathbb{N}$, and observed outcomes $\omega_i \in \Omega$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, the distinction from data remains essential:

\[ \mathbb{P}(A) = \text{value assigned by the model}, \qquad \widehat{p}_n(A) = \text{value computed from the sample} \]
