* Definition:  Sesquilinear/Bilinear form
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:sesquilinear-bilinear-form
:LECTURE_REF: 3.1
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space. We say that a mapping $a : E \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$ is sesquilinear (resp. bilinear) if it is:

- Linear in the first variable,

- Conjugate linear (resp. linear) in the second variable.

In other words, for all $x, y \in E$ and all $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$,

\begin{align*}
a(\lambda x + \mu y, z) &= \lambda a(x, z) + \mu a(y, z) \\
a(x, \lambda y + \mu z) &= \bar{\lambda} a(x, y) + \bar{\mu} a(x, z) \\
\end{align*}

** Questions

*** DONE What does prefix "sesqui" in "sesquilinear" mean?

"sesqui-" from Latin - "one and a half".

*** DONE What does "resp." mean?

Respectively.

*** DONE If sesquiential mapping would also be positive-definite, would it be an inner product?

Close. It would also need to be conjugate symmetric.

*** DONE I find the definition confusing as it just mixes sesquilinear and bilinear forms. Don't you think so as well? Could you define both of them separately?

Yes, the combined form is confusing: it saves a few lines at the cost of forcing the reader to mentally branch on "(resp. ...)" everywhere. The second equation as written is only the sesquilinear case; the bilinear case is left implicit. Separated:

*Bilinear form.* Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space. A mapping $b : E \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$ is bilinear if it is linear in both variables. For all $x, y, z \in E$ and $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$:

$$b(\lambda x + \mu y, z) = \lambda b(x, z) + \mu b(y, z)$$

$$b(x, \lambda y + \mu z) = \lambda b(x, y) + \mu b(x, z)$$

*Sesquilinear form.* Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{C}$ vector space. A mapping $s : E \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ is sesquilinear if it is linear in the first variable and conjugate-linear in the second. For all $x, y, z \in E$ and $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{C}$:

$$s(\lambda x + \mu y, z) = \lambda s(x, z) + \mu s(y, z)$$

$$s(x, \lambda y + \mu z) = \bar{\lambda} s(x, y) + \bar{\mu} s(x, z)$$

Field caveat: the bilinear definition works over any field. The sesquilinear definition genuinely requires $\mathbb{C}$ (or some field with a non-trivial involution) — over $\mathbb{R}$ conjugation is the identity, so the two collapse. The source's blanket "$\mathbb{K}$ vector space" obscures this.

* Definition:  Spaces of bilinear and sesquilinear maps
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:bilinear-sesquilinear-spaces
:LECTURE_REF: 3.2
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space. We denote by

\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Bil}(E) &:= \{ b : E \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{K} \ : \ b \text{ is bilinear} \} \\
\mathrm{Sesq}(E) &:= \{ b : E \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{K} \ : \ b \text{ is sesquilinear} \} \\
\end{align*}

the sets of bilinear and sesquilinear maps.

* Lemma:       Matrix representation in finite dimensional spaces
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:bilinear-matrix-representation
:LECTURE_REF: 3.5
:END:

Let $E$ be $n$ -dimensional $\mathbb{K}$ vector space, $\mathcal{E} = \{ e_1, \ldots, e_p \}$ be a basis, and $b \in \mathrm{Bil}(E)$ and $s \in \mathrm{Sesq}(E)$. It holds that

$$b(x, y) = ([x]^{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} [y]^{\mathcal{E}}, \quad s(x, y) = ([x]^{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [s]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} \overline{[y]^{\mathcal{E}}}, \quad \forall x, y \in E. \tag{3.2}$$

where

$$[b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} := (b(e_i, e_j))_{1 \le i, j \le n}, \quad [s]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} := (s(e_i, e_j))_{1 \le i, j \le n}$$

are called the matrix representation of $b$ and $s$.

#+begin_proof
Let $s \in \mathrm{Sesq}(E)$, and $x, y \in E$. It holds that

$$s(x, y) = \sum_{1 \le i, j \le n} ([x]_{\mathcal{E}})_i \overline{([y]_{\mathcal{E}})_j} s(e_i, e_j) = ([x]^{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [s]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} \overline{[y]^{\mathcal{E}}}$$
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Uniqueness of matrix representation
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:bilinear-matrix-uniqueness
:LECTURE_REF: 3.6
:END:

Let $E$ be $n$ -dimensional $\mathbb{K}$ vector space and $\mathcal{E} = \{ e_1, \ldots, e_p \}$ be a basis. The mappings

$$\mathrm{BMat} : \mathrm{Bil}(E) \rightarrow \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}, \quad b \mapsto [b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}}$$

and

$$\mathrm{SMat} : \mathrm{Sesq}(E) \rightarrow \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}, \quad s \mapsto [s]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}}$$

are isomorphisms.

#+begin_proof
We next prove that =BMat= is linear, surjective and injective.

*Injectivity:* Let $b, \tilde{b} \in \mathrm{Bil}(e_i, e_j)$ such that $[b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} = [\tilde{b}]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}}$. For any $x, y \in E$, we have that

$$b(x, y) = ([x]_{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} [y]_{\mathcal{E}} = ([x]_{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [\tilde{b}]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} [y]_{\mathcal{E}} = b(x, y).$$

Therefore $b = \tilde{b}$ and =BMat= is injective.

*Surjectivity:* Let $A \in \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$, and define $b_A : E \times E \rightarrow \mathbb{K}$ such that $b(x, y) := ([x]_{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} A [y]_{\mathcal{E}}$. We can readily verify that $b \in \mathrm{Bil}(E)$, and this shows that =BMat= is surjective.

The proof for the sesquilinear case follows the same lines, with due care for complex conjugation.

*Linearity:* Trivial.
#+end_proof

* Lemma:       Matrix representation under change of basis
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:bilinear-matrix-change-of-basis
:LECTURE_REF: 3.7
:END:

Let $E$ be $n$ -dimensional $\mathbb{K}$ vector space, and $b \in \mathrm{Bil}(E)$ and $s \in \mathrm{Sesq}(E)$. Then the matrix representation of $b$ and $s$ for two different bases $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{U}$ of $E$ are connected as

$$[b]_{\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}} = ([\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} [\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}}, \quad \text{and} \quad [s]_{\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}} = ([\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [s]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} \overline{[\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}}}$$

#+begin_proof
Let $x, y \in E$. By the matrix representation of $b(x, y)$ (see Eq. (3.2)),

\begin{align*}
b(x, y) &= ([x]^{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} [y]^{\mathcal{E}} \\
&= ([x]^{\mathcal{U}})^{\top} ([\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} [\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}} [y]^{\mathcal{U}} \quad \text{(since } [x]^{\mathcal{E}} = [\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}} [x]^{\mathcal{U}} \text{)} \\
\end{align*}

In addition, since Eq. (3.2) also applies for the basis $\mathcal{U}$, we also have

$$b(x, y) = ([x]^{\mathcal{U}})^{\top} [b]_{\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}} [y]^{\mathcal{U}}$$

Therefore, $[b]_{\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}} = ([\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}})^{\top} [b]_{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}} [\mathrm{Id}]_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{E}}$. We proceed similarly to prove the claim for $s \in \mathrm{Sesq}(E)$.
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Tensor product: axioms
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:tensor-product-axioms
:LECTURE_REF: 3.8
:END:

The tensor product operation $\otimes$ maps a pair of vectors $(x, y) \in E \times E$ to an object called the tensor product $x \otimes y$. We can add tensors and scale them. The product satisfies the following properties:

1. sesquilinearity: for all $x, y, z \in E$ and $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{K}$

   \begin{align*}
   (\lambda x + \mu y) \otimes z &= \lambda x \otimes z + \mu y \otimes z \\
   x \otimes (\lambda y + \mu z) &= \bar{\lambda} x \otimes y + \bar{\mu} x \otimes z \\
   \end{align*}

2. interaction with the zero vector: for all $x, y \in E$,

   $$x \otimes 0 = 0 \otimes 0 = 0 \otimes y$$

3. faithfulness: for $x, y \in E$, if

   $$x \otimes y = 0 \otimes 0,$$

   then either $x = 0$ or $y = 0$.

* Definition:  Tensor product space
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:tensor-product-space
:LECTURE_REF: 3.11
:END:

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space. We define its tensor product space as

$$E \otimes E := \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{r} \alpha_i x_i \otimes y_i \ : \ r \in \mathbb{N}, \alpha_i \in \mathbb{K}, x_i, y_i \in E \ \text{ for } \ 1 \le i \le r \right\}$$

* Lemma:       Tensor product space is a vector space
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lem:tensor-product-vector-space
:LECTURE_REF: 3.12
:END:

$E \otimes E$ is a $\mathbb{K}$ vector space with neutral element $0_{E \otimes E} = 0_E \otimes 0_E$.

#+begin_proof
It holds that $0_{E \otimes E}$ is indeed the neutral element, and that for all $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \in \mathbb{K}$ and $T_1, T_2 \in E \otimes E$, $\lambda_1 T_1 + \lambda_2 T_2 \in E \otimes E$.
#+end_proof

* Definition:  Tensor product space as algebraic dual
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: def:tensor-product-algebraic-dual
:LECTURE_REF: 3.13
:END:

Let $E$ be a vector space. The tensor product space $E \otimes E$ is the algebraic dual of the space of bilinear forms $\mathrm{Bil}(E)$.
